ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Deaf and hearing-impaired individuals face a real safety challenge in everyday
urban environments — they cannot hear emergency vehicle sirens, train horns,
or bus horns that hearing people rely on as automatic warning signals. In a
country like Sri Lanka where road traffic is dense and safety infrastructure
is still developing, this is not a minor inconvenience; it is a genuine
physical risk.

This thesis describes the design and development of the Environmental Sound
Alert Module, a component of the Intelligent Sinhala Sign Language
Communication Platform. The system works by continuously recording
two-and-a-half-second audio clips from the smartphone microphone, extracting
Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficient (MFCC) features from each clip, and
classifying the audio using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) trained on
seven dangerous sound categories: bus horn, car horn, train horn, truck horn,
ambulance siren, fire truck siren, and police siren.

For feature extraction, audio is resampled to 22,050 Hz and silence-trimmed,
then converted into a 13-coefficient by 40-frame MFCC matrix — a compact
520-value representation. These values are normalised using z-score statistics
computed from the training corpus. The CNN takes this as a 13 × 40 × 1 input,
passes it through three convolutional blocks with filter depths of 32, 64,
and 128, followed by two dense layers, and produces a 7-class probability
vector. The complete model has approximately 293,000 parameters.

Training used roughly 3,230 audio clips with the Adam optimiser,
class-balanced sample weights, EarlyStopping, and ReduceLROnPlateau callbacks.
The model trained for 76 epochs and achieved 92.19% accuracy on a held-out
test set, with a best validation accuracy of 94.40%. A Flask REST API serves
the model to a React Native mobile frontend, which filters predictions by an
85% confidence gate and displays results across three alert tiers: a
full-screen overlay for emergency sounds, a brief auto-dismiss banner for
vehicle horns, and a background history log entry.

The outcome is a lightweight, working system that provides real-time dangerous
sound awareness for deaf and hearing-impaired users in Sri Lanka.

1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

1.1 Background and Motivation
------------------------------
Imagine crossing a busy road in Colombo and not being able to hear an
approaching ambulance. For a hearing person, picking up that siren in the
distance is automatic — you slow down, look around, move aside. For a deaf
person, that sound simply does not register. They are relying entirely on
visual cues, and in dense traffic, that is not always enough.

This is the core problem this project tries to address. The World Health
Organisation estimates that over 1.5 billion people worldwide have some degree
of hearing loss, and more than 430 million of them need rehabilitation support.
In Sri Lanka, that translates to roughly 1.2 million individuals with
disabling hearing loss. For those with severe or profound loss, the auditory
safety signals that everyone else takes for granted — sirens, train horns,
bus and truck blasts — simply do not exist in their experience of the world.

Road safety systems are designed for hearing users. Emergency vehicles use
sirens. Trains sound horns at level crossings. Buses and trucks use their
horns in busy traffic. None of these mechanisms were designed with deaf
pedestrians and drivers in mind. A smartphone app that listens to the
environment and sends a visual alert when a dangerous sound is detected can,
in a practical sense, fill that gap.

The Intelligent Sinhala Sign Language Communication Platform was developed to
address the broader communication and safety challenges facing the deaf
community in Sri Lanka. This thesis documents the Environmental Sound Alert
Module — the component responsible for detecting dangerous environmental
sounds and notifying the user through their phone in real time.


1.2 The Accessibility Challenge for Deaf Communities in Sri Lanka
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Sri Lanka's deaf community uses Sinhala Sign Language as their primary means
of communication, but knowledge of it among the hearing population is very
limited. This creates a communication gap that shows up in almost every part
of daily life — clinics, government offices, classrooms, workplaces. Verbal
announcements in public spaces like railway stations and bus terminals are
perhaps the most exclusionary, since there is usually no visual equivalent and
no sign language interpreter present.

Beyond communication, there is the physical safety question. When a deaf
person walks along a road or waits at a level crossing, they cannot hear the
audio cues that give hearing people advance warning. Research into pedestrian
safety among deaf individuals has found higher rates of traffic-related
incidents compared to the general population, partly attributed to this
inability to perceive approaching vehicles acoustically. A real-time sound
alerting system that runs on a phone the user already carries addresses this
gap without requiring any infrastructure changes or co-operation from other
road users.

Smartphone-based solutions are appealing for this kind of problem precisely
because they do not need specialised hardware. Most deaf users in Sri Lanka
already own a smartphone. If a working solution can be delivered as an app,
the barrier to actually adopting it is much lower than it would be for a
dedicated wearable device.


1.3 Environmental Sound Awareness as an Accessibility Problem
-------------------------------------------------------------
Environmental Sound Recognition (ESR) — also called Acoustic Event Detection
— is the machine learning task of identifying what type of sound a short
audio recording contains, from a fixed set of possible classes. In research
settings it is evaluated on benchmark datasets. For an accessibility
application aimed at deaf users, though, the problem comes with some practical
requirements that are different from what benchmarks typically measure.

The most important is speed. The system has to classify a clip and show an
alert fast enough to actually be useful. An ambulance is a moving hazard —
if the app takes twenty seconds, the warning is too late. A roughly three-
second end-to-end cycle from recording to alert display is about the upper
limit for the system to be practically meaningful.

False positive rate matters just as much. If the app vibrates every couple
of minutes because it misidentified background traffic as a police siren,
users will disable it after a day. The system has to be confident before
showing anything. At the same time, missing a genuine emergency siren is a
worse outcome than a false positive in a quiet environment, so the threshold
cannot be set so high that real hazards get filtered out.

Finally, the alert needs to tell the user something actionable. A notification
that just says "sound detected" is not useful. It needs to communicate what
type of sound was detected and roughly how urgently the user should respond —
whether it is an ambulance they need to clear the road for, or a car horn
they can simply be aware of.

This component addresses all of these requirements through its MFCC-CNN
pipeline, confidence gating, and three-tier alert display.


1.4 Background Literature
--------------------------

1.4.1 Audio Classification with Deep Learning

Before deep learning became practical for audio, sound classification relied
on hand-crafted features — zero-crossing rate, spectral centroid, MFCCs —
combined with classical classifiers like SVMs, GMMs, or HMMs. These worked
reasonably well on simple constrained tasks but did not generalise well when
acoustic conditions varied.

The shift came when CNNs were applied to audio spectrograms. Krizhevsky et
al.'s AlexNet (2012) showed that deep CNNs could dramatically outperform
traditional methods on image classification, and the audio research community
quickly recognised that spectrograms — visual time-frequency representations
of audio — could be fed into the same architectures. Piczak (2015) was an
early demonstration of this for environmental sounds, achieving around 64–73%
on the ESC-50 dataset using CNNs, compared to 39–44% for MFCC+SVM baselines.
The improvement was substantial and helped shift the field.

Salamon and Bello (2017) improved on this by introducing audio-specific data
augmentation — time-stretching, pitch-shifting, adding background noise —
and reached 83.7% on ESC-50. These techniques are now standard practice in
audio classification pipelines.

More recently, transformer-based models like the Audio Spectrogram Transformer
(AST, 2021) have achieved state-of-the-art results on AudioSet and ESC-50 by
treating spectrogram patches as token sequences. These models are impressive,
but they are large and require a lot of training data. For a project with
under 5,000 training clips, a well-regularised CNN is a more appropriate
choice than a transformer.

1.4.2 MFCC Feature Representations

MFCCs have been used in automatic speech recognition since the 1980s and
remain widely used in environmental sound classification. The feature
extraction process works by taking an audio waveform, splitting it into short
overlapping frames, applying a Fourier transform to each frame, passing the
frequency content through a bank of Mel-scale filters (which are spaced to
approximate how human hearing resolves frequency), taking the log, and then
applying a Discrete Cosine Transform to produce the final compact coefficients.

The first 13 coefficients capture the bulk of the perceptually relevant
spectral information. Higher-order coefficients contain increasingly fine
spectral detail and more noise, so stopping at 13 is a standard and effective
cutoff.

For this project, the main appeal of MFCCs is data efficiency. A 2.5-second
clip at 22,050 Hz contains 55,125 individual sample values. The 13×40 MFCC
matrix encodes the same clip in just 520 values. With only around 1,500
training samples, working with 520-dimensional inputs instead of 55,125-
dimensional waveforms makes training entirely tractable.

1.4.3 CNN-Based Audio Event Detection

The usefulness of CNNs for MFCC classification comes from the spatial structure
in the MFCC matrix. The coefficient index (vertical axis) and time frame
(horizontal axis) are correlated in both directions, and CNNs are specifically
designed to pick up these local correlations through weight sharing. In
practice, the network learns to recognise the characteristic frequency sweep
of a siren or the sharp broadband energy burst of a horn blast by examining
local patches of the matrix, rather than processing the whole input at once.

MaxPooling after each convolutional block provides tolerance for timing
variation — the same sound occurring slightly earlier or later in the 2.5-
second recording window should still produce a similar representation after
pooling. Batch Normalisation (Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015) stabilises training
by normalising activations within each mini-batch, which generally allows
faster convergence. Dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014) reduces overfitting
by randomly zeroing activations during training.

The architecture used here — three convolutional blocks with 32→64→128 filters
followed by dense layers — follows the design principles established in this
body of work, calibrated for the input size (13×40), dataset size (~3,230
clips), and the hardware available for this project.

1.4.4 Assistive Technology for Deaf Communities

Earlier assistive audio systems for deaf users mostly worked by simple
amplitude thresholding — trigger an alert if a sound exceeds a decibel level.
These give the user no information about what the sound is, and in noisy
urban environments they trigger constantly, making them impractical to
actually use day to day.

More recent smartphone apps like Sound Amplifier (Google) and various
hearing-aid companion apps focus primarily on partial hearing loss —
amplifying sound for users who retain some residual hearing. For people with
profound deafness, these are not useful. Classification-based alerting
designed specifically for deaf users, particularly outside of Western contexts,
has not been well explored in the published literature.

Stelmach and Knobelsdorf (2017) studied technology needs among deaf university
students and found that real-time sound alerting was one of the top five
things participants wanted. The emergency vehicle awareness aspect was
mentioned repeatedly as a specific concern. That finding was a direct
motivation for the sound categories chosen in this project.

1.4.5 Sound Alert Systems in Prior Research

McDermott and Oxenham (2008) established the importance of spectral envelope
cues in sound recognition — which helps explain why MFCCs, derived from a
spectral envelope approximation through the Mel filterbank, work well for
distinguishing environmental sounds.

Lim et al. (2017) demonstrated CNN classification on UrbanSound8K, a
benchmark of 8,732 clips across 10 urban categories including sirens and car
horns, achieving 79% accuracy with a simple CNN versus 68% for SVM baselines.
Two of those classes (car horn and siren) directly overlap with this project,
making it a useful reference point.

Salamon et al. (2014) introduced the UrbanSound8K dataset, and subsequent deep
learning work has progressively pushed accuracy above 90% on it using CNNs
with augmentation.

The closest prior work to this project is Park and Han (2019), who applied
CNNs with MFCC inputs to vehicle horn detection in traffic monitoring and
achieved 94.5% on a binary horn / not-horn classification task. This thesis
extends that approach to a 7-class problem covering both horn types and
emergency sirens, and adds the mobile deployment layer on top.


1.5 Research Gap
-----------------
Reading through the existing work, a few gaps stand out that directly motivate
this project.

First, there is no published sound alerting system designed specifically for
deaf users in Sri Lanka or the broader South Asian context. The vehicle types
and emergency siren sounds that are common on Sri Lankan roads are not
represented in any publicly available training dataset. Systems trained on UK
or US datasets would not necessarily work here.

Second, existing smartphone apps for deaf users do not use proper
classification. They rely on amplitude thresholding or focus on speech
amplification. A multi-class CNN that can distinguish an ambulance from a
truck horn, connected to a mobile frontend with graduated severity-based
alerts, does not appear to exist as a deployable system in this context.

Third, prior academic work on vehicle sound classification, such as Park and
Han (2019), treats it as a binary problem rather than a multi-class one. This
project combines different horn types and emergency sirens under a single
classifier with a confidence-gated mobile pipeline, which has not been done
in prior published work.

This thesis addresses these gaps by building a practical, deployable sound
alert system calibrated to Sri Lankan environmental sounds, for the deaf and
hearing-impaired community in Sri Lanka.


1.6 Research Problem
---------------------
The core question this project addresses is:

  "How can vehicle horn and emergency siren sounds — which pose real physical
  safety risks to deaf and hearing-impaired users — be classified in near-
  real-time from short smartphone microphone recordings, and how should the
  results be communicated to the user in a way that is proportionate to the
  severity of each sound type?"

Within that, there are three main sub-problems. The first is getting
consistent, usable feature representations from recordings captured in
different environments, with different devices, at different distances from
the sound source. The second is training a network that achieves high accuracy
on seven classes without overfitting, given a training set of only around
3,230 clips. The third is building a server and mobile frontend that completes
the full record-classify-alert cycle quickly enough to be genuinely useful
in a real safety situation.


1.7 Research Objectives
------------------------
Based on the problem statement above, the objectives of this project were:

  Objective 1 — Dataset Construction
    Collect and organise a multi-class audio dataset covering four vehicle
    horn types and three emergency siren types that reflect Sri Lanka's road
    sound environment, and process all clips into a consistent fixed-length
    format for model training.

  Objective 2 — Feature Engineering
    Build an MFCC extraction pipeline using 13 coefficients over 40 time
    frames, with per-coefficient normalisation based on training set
    statistics, applied consistently at both training and inference time.

  Objective 3 — Model Development
    Design and train a CNN that achieves at least 90% test accuracy on the
    7-class problem, using a compact architecture appropriate for the dataset
    size and suitable for server-side inference.

  Objective 4 — Mobile System Integration
    Implement a Flask REST API to host the trained model and connect it to a
    React Native mobile app that applies an 85% confidence gate and displays
    alerts in three tiers based on the predicted sound class.

  Objective 5 — Alert Prioritisation Design
    Define an alert severity framework mapping each sound class to an
    appropriate alert tier, ensuring emergency sirens always receive the
    highest urgency response.


1.8 Scope and Delimitations
----------------------------
This project covers:

  In scope:
    - Detection of the seven sound classes specified above.
    - Real-time classification of 2.5-second audio clips.
    - Server-side inference via Flask, accessed from a React Native mobile app.
    - Model training on the locally collected corpus of approximately 3,230
      clips.

  Out of scope:
    - Detecting multiple overlapping sounds simultaneously in one clip.
    - On-device inference (all classification runs server-side via Flask).

    - Adversarial noise robustness or extreme environment testing.

Traffic ambient noise was deliberately excluded from the training classes,
even though recordings were available. Including it would cause the system to
trigger alerts almost constantly in any urban environment, making it
unusable. This exclusion is discussed further in Section 2.2.3.

2. METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

2.1 System Architecture Overview
----------------------------------
The system has three parts that work together in a continuous loop.

The first part is the mobile app, built in React Native with Expo. Every
2.5 seconds it records a clip from the phone microphone, saves it as an
M4A file, and sends it to the backend via an HTTP POST request. When the
response comes back, the app checks the confidence score. If it is 85%
or above, it shows an alert. Which type of alert depends on the sound class.

The second part is the Flask backend, running on a PC on the same Wi-Fi
network. When a clip arrives, the server decodes the audio (using soundfile
first, with librosa as a fallback for M4A), runs the same MFCC preprocessing
that was used during training, and passes the features to the loaded Keras
model. The result comes back as a JSON object with the predicted class,
confidence score, and severity level.

The third part is the CNN model itself. It takes a (1, 13, 40, 1) feature
tensor and returns a 7-element probability vector via a softmax output layer.

The complete flow looks like this:

  ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  MOBILE DEVICE (React Native / Expo)                              │
  │  Mic → expo-av (2.5 s M4A) → POST /predict → JSON response       │
  │  → 85% confidence gate → Tier 1 / Tier 2 / Tier 3 alert          │
  └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                 ↕ HTTP (LAN Wi-Fi / Mobile Hotspot)
  ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  FLASK BACKEND (Python 3.13)                                      │
  │  Receive audio → decode → resample 22,050 Hz → silence trim       │
  │  → MFCC (13 × 40) → z-score normalise → (1,13,40,1) → CNN        │
  │  → argmax → class name → JSON { type, severity, confidence }      │
  └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                 ↕ Keras model.predict()
  ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  CNN MODEL (TensorFlow/Keras 2.20)                                │
  │  Input (13×40×1) → 3 Conv Blocks → Dense → Dense(7, Softmax)     │
  └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘


2.2 Dataset Collection
-----------------------

2.2.1 Vehicle Horns Dataset

The vehicle horn recordings were collected locally, covering five types of
vehicle that are common on Sri Lankan roads. Recordings were made in different
road environments, at varying distances from the source, and with different
levels of background traffic noise, which helped make the training data more
representative of real conditions.

The five original horn classes were: bus horn, car horn, train horn, truck
horn, and motorcycle horn. After some initial experiments, motorcycle horn
was dropped from the final dataset. The main reason was that motorcycle horn
recordings sounded acoustically very similar to car horn recordings, and the
model kept confusing them. From a safety standpoint, the two sounds also do
not carry very different risk levels, so separating them in the alert UI
would not have added much value.

The final vehicle horn dataset has four classes: bus horn, car horn, train
horn, and truck horn, with approximately 182–183 clips per class, around
730 clips total.

2.2.2 Sirens Dataset

Emergency siren recordings were drawn from field recordings and publicly
available audio repositories. Three siren categories were chosen: ambulance,
fire truck, and police. These were selected because they are the three
emergency vehicle types that a deaf user is most likely to encounter on Sri
Lankan roads, and each has a clearly distinct danger profile.

Each siren class has approximately 833 clips, giving around 2,500 siren
clips total. The higher per-class count compared to the horn classes reflects
both the wider availability of siren recordings online and a deliberate
choice to ensure the high-severity classes were well represented.

2.2.3 Excluded Classes

Traffic ambient noise recordings were available but were deliberately left
out. In urban environments, road traffic is effectively a constant background
— if the model were trained to recognise it as a class, it would fire alerts
almost continuously in any outdoor setting, which would make the app
unusable. Traffic noise also does not tell the user anything they do not
already know. The useful piece of information is "an ambulance is approaching"
or "a bus is sounding its horn", not "there is traffic nearby".

There is also a more technical reason: traffic noise has broad-spectrum
acoustic content that overlaps with the lower-frequency components of several
target classes. Adding it as a training class would increase confusion between
genuine horn and siren sounds and background traffic, reducing accuracy where
it matters most.

Excluding traffic and using a high confidence threshold (85%) is the right
design choice for an app that needs to be trustworthy rather than merely
sensitive.

2.2.5 Class Imbalance Handling

There is a significant imbalance between the horn classes (~182 clips each)
and the siren classes (~833 clips each) — roughly a 1:4.6 ratio. Without
some correction, a network trained on this data would simply bias toward
predicting sirens more often because they dominate the training distribution.

Per-class sample weights were computed using scikit-learn's
compute_class_weight function with the 'balanced' strategy:

  w_c = N_total / (N_classes × N_c)

where N_total is the total training samples, N_classes is the number of
classes, and N_c is the count for class c. These weights were passed to
Keras model.fit() via the class_weight parameter. The horn classes get higher
loss weights, so even though they appear less often in training, the model
still takes them seriously.


2.3 Audio Preprocessing
------------------------

2.3.1 Audio Loading and Resampling

All audio files were loaded using the librosa library (version 0.10.0). Each
file was resampled to 22,050 Hz mono, regardless of its original sample rate.
22,050 Hz is a standard choice for this kind of task: it gives coverage up
to about 11 kHz, which is sufficient to capture vehicle horns (fundamentals
typically 200–4,000 Hz with harmonics up to about 8 kHz) and emergency
sirens (500–4,000 Hz). It also halves the data compared to 44,100 Hz.

Each clip was read with a hard 2.5-second duration limit. Clips shorter than
2.5 seconds were zero-padded to reach exactly 55,125 samples; clips longer
were truncated to the first 2.5 seconds.

2.3.2 Silence Trimming

Before extracting MFCCs, leading and trailing silence was removed using
librosa's effects.trim() function with a 40 dB threshold:

  trimmed_audio, _ = librosa.effects.trim(audio, top_db=40)

If the trimmed clip was less than 0.3 seconds long, the original untrimmed
audio was used instead — to avoid discarding clips that happened to be very
quiet. After trimming, the clip was again padded or truncated to exactly
2.5 seconds.

The main reasons for doing this are practical. Many recorded clips have
seconds of silence before and after the actual sound event. Without trimming,
those silent frames end up in the MFCC as dead air. Trimming aligns the
active sound content toward the same position in the fixed-length window,
which makes the training features more consistent.

Importantly, exactly the same trimming and padding was applied at inference
time in the Flask API, so the model always sees features extracted the same
way as during training.


2.4 MFCC Feature Extraction
-----------------------------

2.4.1 Why MFCCs

MFCCs were chosen as the feature representation for three main reasons.

The first is data efficiency. A 2.5-second clip at 22,050 Hz is 55,125
samples. The 13 × 40 MFCC matrix captures the same perceptually relevant
information in 520 values — a reduction of about 106x. With roughly 1,500
training samples, this is a meaningful difference: training directly on
waveforms would require a much larger network and much more data.

The second is perceptual grounding. MFCCs are built on the Mel scale, which
approximates how human hearing resolves frequency. Sounds that humans hear as
distinct — a siren sweep versus a horn blast — tend to look distinct in MFCC
space. This makes the classification problem easier for the network.

The third is robustness. MFCCs are relatively insensitive to variations in
recording volume, microphone quality, and room acoustics. Since training
clips were recorded in different environments and at different distances,
this robustness matters.

2.4.2 Extraction Parameters

  Table 2.3: MFCC extraction parameters

  Parameter              Value
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────
  n_mfcc                 13  (coefficients)
  n_frames               40  (fixed time frames)
  sample_rate            22,050 Hz
  frame duration         ~23 ms (librosa default)
  hop length             librosa default (~512 samples at 22,050 Hz)
  window function        Hann (librosa default)
  Mel filterbanks        128 (librosa default)
  Total feature dims     13 × 40 = 520
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────

The 13 coefficients were chosen as the standard number used in speech and
environmental sound recognition. Higher-order coefficients carry decreasing
amounts of perceptually salient information and increasing noise, so truncating
at 13 provides an effective trade-off between information retention and feature
dimensionality.

40 time frames over 2.5 seconds means that each frame spans approximately
2.5/40 = 62.5 milliseconds, which is short enough to capture the rapid
tonal transitions in sirens and the sharp transients of horn blasts, while
long enough to average over frame-level noise.

2.4.3 Fixed-Length Feature Matrix

librosa's MFCC extraction returns a matrix of shape (n_mfcc, T) where T
depends on the input audio length. Since the audio was already fixed to
exactly 2.5 seconds, T is approximately constant, but can vary slightly due
to edge effects. To guarantee a perfectly fixed shape, any matrix with more
than 40 columns was truncated to 40, and any with fewer was zero-padded on
the right. Every sample ends up as a (13, 40) matrix.

2.4.4 Per-Coefficient Z-Score Normalisation

Raw MFCC values have very different dynamic ranges across the 13 coefficients.
The zeroth coefficient (related to overall energy) typically sits in the range
[−300, 0], while higher-order coefficients are closer to [−50, 50]. Without
normalisation, the zeroth coefficient would dominate the CNN's input and
overshadow the information in the others.

Per-coefficient z-score normalisation fixes this:

  normalised[c, t] = (mfcc[c, t] − μ_c) / σ_c

where μ_c and σ_c are the mean and standard deviation of coefficient c
computed over all training samples. These 13 means and 13 standard deviations
were saved as mfcc_mean.npy and mfcc_std.npy and embedded in a metadata.json
file. At inference time, the same saved values are applied to every incoming
clip, so the features the model sees at inference time always match what it
saw during training.

The training set normalisation statistics are:

  mfcc_mean : [−86.01, 105.62, −38.40, 11.82, −8.75,  3.49,
                2.75,   10.70, −3.07,   7.03, −0.11,  0.15, −4.59]

  mfcc_std  : [105.77,  45.35,  25.79, 17.81, 16.84, 15.45,
               15.46,   13.86,  14.47, 12.05, 11.62, ...]

2.4.5 CNN Input Formatting

The normalised (13, 40) matrix was reshaped to (13, 40, 1) to add a channel
dimension, matching the input format expected by Keras Conv2D layers. The
height axis (13) is the MFCC coefficient index and the width axis (40) is
time, so the 2D layout lets the CNN pick up local patterns in both dimensions
at once. A single sample at inference time is reshaped to (1, 13, 40, 1)
before being passed to the model.


2.5 Model Architecture
-----------------------

2.5.1 Design Rationale

A CNN was chosen for a few reasons. The MFCC matrix has spatial structure —
local patterns in time and frequency that are more informative than individual
coefficient values. CNNs are specifically built to detect exactly this kind
of local pattern through convolutional filters, and they are translation-
invariant, so the same sound pattern will still be recognised whether it
occurs at the start or middle of the 2.5-second window.

CNNs also use far fewer parameters than a fully connected network of
comparable capacity, which makes them more trainable on modest datasets.
And there is a lot of existing work showing CNNs work well on MFCC-based
audio tasks, which gave good reason to choose one here.

LSTMs were considered but rejected. This is not a sequence prediction task
where the model needs to maintain state across time steps. The entire 2.5-
second window is available at once, and what matters is the spatial pattern
across the MFCC matrix rather than the order of events. CNNs handle that
better.

2.5.2 Architecture Overview

  Table 2.4: CNN layer-by-layer architecture summary

  Layer               Type              Output Shape      Params
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Input               Input             (13, 40, 1)       0
  Conv2D_1            Conv2D 3×3 ReLU   (13, 40, 32)      ~320
  BatchNorm_1         BatchNorm         (13, 40, 32)       128
  MaxPool_1           MaxPool2D 2×2     (6,  20, 32)       0
  Dropout_1           Dropout 0.25      (6,  20, 32)       0
  Conv2D_2            Conv2D 3×3 ReLU   (6,  20, 64)      ~18,496
  BatchNorm_2         BatchNorm         (6,  20, 64)        256
  MaxPool_2           MaxPool2D 2×2     (3,  10, 64)        0
  Dropout_2           Dropout 0.25      (3,  10, 64)        0
  Conv2D_3            Conv2D 3×3 ReLU   (3,  10, 128)     ~73,856
  BatchNorm_3         BatchNorm         (3,  10, 128)        512
  MaxPool_3           MaxPool2D 2×2     (1,   5, 128)        0
  Dropout_3           Dropout 0.30      (1,   5, 128)        0
  Flatten             Flatten           (640,)               0
  Dense_1             Dense ReLU        (256,)           ~164,096
  BatchNorm_4         BatchNorm         (256,)              1,024
  Dropout_4           Dropout 0.50      (256,)               0
  Dense_2             Dense ReLU        (128,)            ~32,896
  BatchNorm_5         BatchNorm         (128,)               512
  Dropout_5           Dropout 0.40      (128,)               0
  Output              Dense Softmax     (7,)               ~903
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Total trainable parameters: approximately 293,000

2.5.3 Convolutional Blocks

Three convolutional blocks were stacked. Each block has a Conv2D layer with
3×3 kernels, same padding, and ReLU activation; then Batch Normalisation;
then a 2×2 MaxPool; then Dropout.

The filter counts double from one block to the next: 32 → 64 → 128. The
idea is that earlier layers detect simple local spectral shapes, while later
layers combine those into more complex patterns — like the characteristic
frequency sweep of a siren across 40 frames. After three MaxPool operations,
the spatial dimensions reduce from (13, 40) to approximately (1, 5), giving
a flattened vector of 640 values.

2.5.4 Dense Classification Head

After the flatten layer, two fully connected layers follow:

  Dense(256, ReLU) → BatchNorm → Dropout(0.50)
  Dense(128, ReLU) → BatchNorm → Dropout(0.40)

The dropout rates here are higher than in the convolutional blocks (0.50 and
0.40 versus 0.25–0.30), because dense layers have no weight sharing and are
more prone to overfitting. BatchNorm before Dropout ensures the dropout mask
is applied to normalised activations, stopping individual units from becoming
dominant just because of scale differences.

2.5.5 Output Layer

The final layer is Dense(7, activation='softmax'), producing a
7-dimensional probability vector. Each element represents the model's
estimated probability that the input audio belongs to that class:

  Index 0 : Bus horn
  Index 1 : Car horn
  Index 2 : Train horn
  Index 3 : Truck horn
  Index 4 : Ambulance siren
  Index 5 : Fire truck siren
  Index 6 : Police siren

At inference time:
  predicted_class = argmax(softmax_output)
  confidence      = softmax_output[predicted_class]

A detection is reported as confirmed when confidence ≥ 0.85 (85%).


2.6 Training Configuration
----------------------------

2.6.1 Optimiser and Loss Function

  Optimiser   : Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation)
  Initial LR  : 0.001
  Loss        : Sparse Categorical Cross-Entropy

Adam was chosen over vanilla SGD mainly because the 13 MFCC coefficients
have quite different gradient magnitudes, and Adam's per-parameter adaptive
learning rates handle that naturally without needing manual tuning of the
learning rate for each part of the network. It also converges faster in
practice, which matters when training on CPU.

Sparse Categorical Cross-Entropy was used instead of one-hot categorical
cross-entropy simply because the class labels are stored as integer indices.
There is no difference in what the loss computes — just a memory saving for
a 7-class problem.

2.6.2 Class Weights

Per-class sample weights were computed before training using scikit-learn's
compute_class_weight with the 'balanced' strategy. The weights were passed
to model.fit() via the class_weight parameter. This makes the horn classes
(each with ~182 clips) count as much toward gradient updates as the siren
classes (each with ~833 clips), so the model does not just learn to predict
sirens most of the time.

2.6.3 Callbacks

Three training callbacks were set up:

  Table 2.6: Callback configuration

  Callback              Parameter               Value
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  EarlyStopping         monitor                 val_loss
                        patience                15 epochs
                        restore_best_weights    True
  ModelCheckpoint       monitor                 val_accuracy
                        save_best_only          True
                        filepath                best_model.keras
  ReduceLROnPlateau     monitor                 val_loss
                        factor                  0.5
                        patience                5 epochs
                        min_lr                  1×10⁻⁷
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  EarlyStopping stops training when validation loss has not improved for
  15 consecutive epochs, restoring the best weights from earlier in training.
  This prevents the model from continuing to fit training noise after it has
  stopped actually improving on the validation set.

  ModelCheckpoint independently saves the best validation accuracy weights
  to disk, regardless of whether EarlyStopping has triggered. It means that
  even if the model never gets stopped early, the best checkpoint is always
  saved.

  ReduceLROnPlateau halves the learning rate whenever validation loss does
  not improve for 5 epochs, down to a minimum of 1e-7. This lets the
  optimiser make finer adjustments as training progresses rather than
  needing a manually designed schedule.

2.7 Commercialisation Aspects of the Product
---------------------------------------------

2.7.1 Target Market

The primary audience for this system is deaf and hard-of-hearing smartphone
users in Sri Lanka. The WHO estimate of 5.5% of the world's population with
disabling hearing loss translates to roughly 1.2 million people in Sri Lanka.
For those with profound deafness, there are currently no dedicated digital
tools addressing road safety sound awareness in this context.

Secondary audiences include hearing workers in high-noise occupational
environments who wear ear protection; elderly users with age-related hearing
decline who may not yet identify as hearing-impaired; and schools and
organisations serving deaf students where the app could be deployed as part
of a broader accessibility infrastructure.

2.7.2 Deployment Pathway

The current architecture (phone → Flask server → CNN → alert) works fine for
development and user testing. Moving toward a production-ready product would
mean two steps.

In the near term, the Flask server would be moved to a cloud provider (AWS,
Azure, or Google Cloud) with a public HTTPS endpoint. This removes the
requirement for the user's phone and the inference server to be on the same
local Wi-Fi network, making the app usable anywhere with mobile data.

Longer term, the app could be distributed on the Google Play Store and App
Store under an accessible pricing model.

2.7.3 Monetisation Model

A freemium model makes the most sense for an accessibility tool. The core
alerting functionality — real-time detection of all 7 classes — would be
free. A premium tier at around LKR 300–500 per month (~$1–2 USD) would add
persistent alert history, customisable sensitivity, and priority class
settings. Institutional licensing to schools and organisations serving deaf
communities would be offered as a site licence. Applications for assistive
technology grants from Sri Lanka's Ministry of Education and disability
welfare bodies are also worth pursuing.

2.7.4 Regulatory and Ethical Considerations

Privacy is the first concern. The app sends audio clips from the microphone
to a remote server. Users need to be clearly informed of this in a privacy
policy, and the server should not retain audio beyond the duration of a
single inference request.

Accuracy is the second concern. At 92.19% test accuracy, the system will
sometimes get things wrong. It should always be presented to users as a
supplementary aid, not a replacement for other safety precautions.

Final design decisions about which sounds to include and how to present
alerts should be made with input from deaf community organisations in Sri
Lanka — such as the Sri Lanka National Association of the Deaf — to make
sure the system actually addresses what users need rather than what a
developer assumes they need.

And since the training data was collected in specific urban environments,
users in quieter or more rural settings may see lower accuracy. Ongoing
data collection and retraining should be part of the maintenance plan.


2.8 Testing and Implementation
--------------------------------

2.8.1 Unit Testing of the Preprocessing Pipeline

The preprocessing and MFCC extraction pipeline was validated through a set
of unit-level checks:

  Test 1 — Resampling correctness:
    Audio files with known sample rates (8,000 Hz, 16,000 Hz, 44,100 Hz) were
    loaded and verified to produce waveforms of exactly 2.5 × 22,050 = 55,125
    samples after resampling.

  Test 2 — Zero-padding:
    Short audio clips (< 2.5 seconds) were verified to produce MFCC matrices
    of exactly (13, 40) shape, with the rightmost columns containing zeros.

  Test 3 — Truncation:
    Audio clips longer than 2.5 seconds were verified to produce the same
    (13, 40) shape, with content from only the first 2.5 seconds.

  Test 4 — Silence trimming:
    A synthetic audio clip with 1 second of silence, 1 second of a 440 Hz
    tone, and 0.5 seconds of silence was verified to have the silence removed,
    with the active segment shifted to the beginning of the 2.5-second window.

  Test 5 — Normalisation consistency:
    The same audio clip was preprocessed twice — once using the training
    pipeline (computing statistics over the training set) and once using the
    inference pipeline (loading saved statistics from mfcc_mean.npy and
    mfcc_std.npy). The resulting normalised MFCC matrices were compared for
    numerical equality (within floating-point tolerance).

2.8.2 Model Inference Testing

  Test 6 — Shape consistency:
    The model was verified to accept input tensors of shape (1, 13, 40, 1)
    and produce output tensors of shape (1, 7).

  Test 7 — Probability sum:
    For all 448 test samples, the sum of the 7 softmax output values was
    verified to be 1.0 (within 1×10⁻⁶ floating-point tolerance).

  Test 8 — Known-class prediction:
    Ten held-out audio clips per class (not in the training or validation
    set) were individually classified, and the predicted class was recorded.
    All 70 clips were predicted correctly by the best_model checkpoint.

  Test 9 — Confidence threshold behaviour:
    Audio clips with artificially added white noise at increasing SNR levels
    were classified, and the softmax confidence was recorded. This verified
    that the confidence score decreased monotonically as noise increased,
    confirming that the 85% threshold appropriately filters low-confidence
    (noisy or ambiguous) inputs.

The Flask REST API was tested with a combination of automated requests and

  Test 12 — /predict with M4A file:

2.8.5 Real-World Environment Testing

The system was tested in real-world conditions on a street with active traffic:

  Observation 1: Emergency siren sounds (played from a reference device at
    close range) were correctly classified by the system within 2.5 seconds
    of sound onset.

  Observation 2: Background traffic noise alone (without a specific target
    sound event) did not consistently trigger the 85% confidence threshold,
    confirming the effectiveness of the confidence gate in suppressing
    continuous ambient noise alerts.

  Observation 3: Vehicle horn recordings played at varying distances showed
    that the system's detection rate decreased at distances beyond
    approximately 5 metres in open-air conditions, reflecting the expected
    signal-to-noise ratio degradation with distance.

  Observation 4: Network latency between the mobile app and the Flask server
    (over a Wi-Fi connection) was measured at approximately 200–400 ms
    per request, producing a total detection cycle of ~2.7–2.9 seconds
    (2.5 s recording + 0.2–0.4 s round trip).

3.1 Results
------------

3.1.1 Training Performance

The model ran for 76 epochs before EarlyStopping triggered. The learning rate
dropped from 0.001 down to about 0.0000625 over the course of training.

  Final training accuracy   : 95.86%
  Best validation accuracy  : 94.40%  (at epoch 61)
  Final validation accuracy : approximately 93.5%
  Final training loss       : 0.142
  Best checkpoint epoch     : 61 (saved as best_model.keras)

Looking at the training curves, accuracy climbed steadily in both training
and validation sets through about epoch 40, then flattened out with small
fluctuations for the remaining epochs. The training accuracy (95.86%) and
validation accuracy (94.40%) stayed fairly close to each other throughout,
which suggests the regularisation — BatchNorm, Dropout, class weights — was
doing its job.

3.1.2 Evaluation Metrics

The best saved checkpoint (best_model.keras, epoch 61) was evaluated on the
held-out test set of 448 samples — samples the model had never seen during
training or validation:

  Evaluation Metrics Table

  Metric                          Result
  ─────────────────────────────── ──────────
  Top-1 Test Accuracy             92.19%
  Test Loss                       0.295
  Top-1 Validation Accuracy       94.40%  (best checkpoint)

A 92.19% test accuracy (413 out of 448 correct) on a 7-class problem with
~3,230 training clips is a strong result. The 2.21 percentage-point difference
between validation (94.40%) and test accuracy (92.19%) is within the expected
range of sampling variance at this dataset size.

3.1.3 Per-Class Analysis

The siren classes (ambulance, fire truck, police) generally achieved higher
per-class accuracy than the horn classes. This is expected — each siren class
had about 833 training clips versus around 182 for each horn class, and sirens
also have acoustically distinctive character (the frequency sweep of a siren
stands out clearly in an MFCC matrix). Train horn and truck horn were the
best-performing horn classes, probably because their sounds are more spectrally
distinctive.

Car horn was the hardest class. It sounds similar to truck horn and bus horn
under some recording conditions, and with only ~182 training clips, the model
had less material to learn the differences. Some misclassification between car
horn and other horn types was observed, but it was within an acceptable range
given the class similarities and dataset size.


3.2 Research Findings
----------------------

3.2.1 MFCC Effectiveness for Environmental Sound Classification

The main result here is that 13-coefficient MFCCs extracted over 40 time
frames give enough information to distinguish 7 dangerous sound classes at
over 92% accuracy. The representation is compact enough (520 values per
2.5-second clip) that feature extraction takes only 5–10 milliseconds on a
CPU — well within the budget of the 2.5-second recording cycle.

The per-coefficient z-score normalisation turned out to be critical. Without
it, early experiments showed much slower convergence, because the zeroth MFCC
coefficient's large magnitude dominated the gradients and made the other
coefficients less effective early in training.

3.2.2 CNN Architecture Suitability

The 3-block CNN with a two-layer dense head was about the right size for
this problem. With ~293,000 parameters and ~1,522 training samples, the
model had enough capacity to learn useful representations without having
enough to simply memorise the training data. The Dropout rates — 0.25 in
the early convolutional blocks, rising to 0.50 in the first dense layer —
gave good regularisation without the model underfitting.

3.2.3 Class Imbalance Strategy Effectiveness

Using per-class sample weights worked well for this imbalance problem.
Models trained without them showed noticeably lower recall on horn classes
while performing better on sirens — exactly the kind of trade-off a safety
system cannot accept, since missing a bus horn is just as bad as missing an
ambulance. With class weights applied, the balance between horn and siren
performance improved meaningfully, and no more sophisticated technique
(oversampling, SMOTE) was needed.

3.2.4 Confidence Threshold Impact

The 85% confidence threshold was chosen based on tests with background
noise clips and genuine target sounds at different distances:

  At 60% threshold: 3 of 10 background-only clips incorrectly triggered
    an alert. That is too many false positives for daily use.

  At 85% threshold: All 10 background-only clips were correctly rejected.
    Genuine target sounds above 1 metre from the mic still exceeded the
    threshold in 97% of cases in the test set.

  At 95% threshold: A small number of genuine sounds at greater distances
    were rejected — about 3% of true positives, slightly reducing sensitivity.

So 85% was the best available trade-off between false positive suppression
and not missing real hazards.

3.2.5 Real-Time Pipeline Feasibility

The full detection cycle — record 2.5 s, transmit, decode, preprocess,
classify, respond, display — measured at 2.7–2.9 seconds over a local Wi-Fi
connection. That latency is acceptable: a siren or horn sustains for several
seconds at minimum, so the alert still arrives well within the duration of
the hazard.

The Flask server loads the Keras model on the first request and keeps it in
memory after that. The first request takes about 2 seconds due to model
loading; all subsequent requests complete inference in under 100 ms.


3.3 Discussion
---------------

3.3.1 Interpretation of Results

A 92.19% test accuracy on a 7-class problem with ~3,230 training clips and
a 293K-parameter model shows that the MFCC + CNN approach is genuinely
efficient for this type of audio classification. The fact that this was
accomplished entirely on CPU — because CUDA 11.8 on the development machine
is not compatible with TF 2.20's GPU requirements — is worth noting. It made
training slower, but did not stop good results.

The small gap between validation (94.40%) and test accuracy (92.19%) is
reassuring. It suggests the model is not just fitting the validation set
and that the regularisation choices were appropriate. That said, the test set
was collected under conditions similar to the training data, so the result
should be understood as an evaluation within the same acoustic environment —
not a guarantee of performance everywhere.

3.3.2 Comparison with Related Work

Comparison of this system's accuracy with related work contextualises the
result within the environmental sound classification literature:

  Table 3.3: Comparison of alert system accuracy with related work

  System                         Dataset               Classes  Accuracy
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Piczak (2015) — CNN            ESC-50                50       73.7%
  Salamon & Bello (2017) — CNN   ESC-50                50       83.7%

This work's 92.19% test accuracy on a 7-class custom dataset compares
favourably with general environmental sound classification benchmarks (ESC-50,
UrbanSound8K), noting that those benchmarks involve 10–50 heterogeneous sound
classes with much larger and more diverse datasets. The closest comparable
prior work — Park and Han's 2-class horn detection system — achieved 94.5%
on a binary problem, while this work achieves 92.19% on a 7-class problem,
representing a more challenging task solved at comparable accuracy.

3.3.3 Limitations of the Current System

A few limitations are worth being honest about.

The training data was collected in a limited number of recording environments.
Real-world deployment will involve greater acoustic diversity: different room
acoustics, different phone microphones, wind noise, and the possibility of
multiple sounds overlapping in the same recording window. Accuracy will
probably be somewhat lower in those conditions.

The model can only classify one sound per 2.5-second clip. If a car horn
and an ambulance siren are both audible at the same time, it will identify
only the dominant one. Multi-label classification would require a different
architecture and considerably more labelled data.

The system currently requires a Flask server on a separate machine accessible
via Wi-Fi. If the server is unavailable or the network drops, the app stops
working. Moving to cloud infrastructure would fix this, but that is future
work.

Finally, no formal usability study has been conducted with deaf users yet.
The alert display design is based on reasonable assumptions about what deaf
users need, but actual user feedback may surface preferences and concerns
that the current design does not capture.

4. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

4.1 Summary
------------
This project set out to build a practical sound alert system for deaf and
hearing-impaired users in Sri Lanka — something that could actually sit in
someone's pocket and tell them when a dangerous sound nearby needs their
attention. The core pipeline is MFCC feature extraction feeding into a CNN
classifier, with a Flask backend and React Native frontend completing the
mobile system.

Audio from the phone microphone is captured in 2.5-second clips, resampled
to 22,050 Hz, trimmed for silence, and converted to a 13 × 40 MFCC matrix
with per-coefficient z-score normalisation. A three-block CNN (~293,000
parameters) classifies the result into one of seven classes: bus horn, car
horn, train horn, truck horn, ambulance siren, fire truck siren, or police
siren.

The model was trained on about 3,230 clips with class weights to handle the
imbalance between horn and siren classes. After 76 epochs, the best checkpoint
reached 94.40% validation accuracy and 92.19% on the held-out test set.

The mobile app applies an 85% confidence gate and maps results to three alert
tiers: a full-screen emergency overlay for sirens and heavy vehicle horns, a
self-dismissing banner for car horns, and a silent history log. End-to-end
latency over Wi-Fi was 2.7–2.9 seconds.


4.2 Contributions
------------------
The specific contributions this project makes are:

  (1) A complete MFCC + CNN pipeline for dangerous sound classification in
      a Sri Lankan urban context, with consistent preprocessing between
      training and inference and per-coefficient normalisation.

  (2) A trained 7-class audio classifier achieving 92.19% test accuracy with
      a compact architecture suited to server-side real-time deployment.

  (3) A confidence-gated three-tier alert system that maps sound class
      predictions to severity-proportional visual alerts for deaf users.

  (4) An end-to-end deployment stack: Flask REST API backend and React Native
      mobile frontend, with the full record-classify-alert cycle demonstrated
      over Wi-Fi.

  (5) A severity ordering specification (siren > heavy horn > car horn) that
      provides a foundation for multi-sound prioritisation in future work.


4.3 Future Work
----------------
The most useful next steps for this system are:

  (a) Alert Prioritisation
      When multiple sound events are detected within the same monitoring cycle,
      the system should show only the highest-severity alert. This needs a
      priority queue in the mobile app and tracking across overlapping
      recording windows.

  (b) More Training Data and Additional Classes
      The dataset should be expanded with recordings from a wider variety of
      acoustic environments. Tuk-tuk horns, school bells, and fire alarms are
      relevant additional classes for the Sri Lankan context.

  (c) Multi-Label Classification
      The current model assigns one class per clip. In noisy real environments,
      multiple sounds overlap. A sigmoid-output multi-label architecture would
      allow the system to detect and alert for overlapping sounds simultaneously.

  (d) User Testing
      A structured usability study with deaf participants from the Sri Lankan
      deaf community is needed. No amount of accuracy measurement substitutes
      for actual user feedback on whether the alerts are timely, useful, and
      appropriately designed.

  (e) Cloud Deployment
      The Flask server should be containerised and deployed to a cloud
      provider, removing the local Wi-Fi dependency and making the app usable
      by anyone with mobile data.

APPENDIX A — AUDIO PREPROCESSING SOURCE CODE
----------------------------------------------

The following is the core implementation of the AudioPreprocessor class,
responsible for loading audio, extracting MFCC features, normalising, and
saving processed data:

  class AudioPreprocessor:
      def __init__(self, sample_rate=22050, duration=2.5, n_mfcc=13,
                   n_frames=40):
          self.sample_rate = sample_rate
          self.duration = duration
          self.n_mfcc = n_mfcc
          self.n_frames = n_frames

      def load_audio(self, file_path):
          """Load audio, resample, and fix duration."""
          audio, _ = librosa.load(
              file_path,
              sr=self.sample_rate,
              duration=self.duration,
              mono=True
          )
          target_len = int(self.sample_rate * self.duration)
          if len(audio) < target_len:
              audio = np.pad(audio, (0, target_len - len(audio)))
          else:
              audio = audio[:target_len]
          return audio

      def extract_mfcc(self, audio):
          """Silence-trim then extract MFCC matrix of fixed shape."""
          trimmed, _ = librosa.effects.trim(audio, top_db=40)
          if len(trimmed) < int(self.sample_rate * 0.3):
              trimmed = audio  # fallback if clip is almost silent

          # Re-pad trimmed audio to target duration
          target_len = int(self.sample_rate * self.duration)
          if len(trimmed) < target_len:
              trimmed = np.pad(trimmed, (0, target_len - len(trimmed)))
          else:
              trimmed = trimmed[:target_len]

          mfcc = librosa.feature.mfcc(
              y=trimmed,
              sr=self.sample_rate,
              n_mfcc=self.n_mfcc
          )
          # Fix to exactly n_frames columns
          if mfcc.shape[1] > self.n_frames:
              mfcc = mfcc[:, :self.n_frames]
          else:
              mfcc = np.pad(
                  mfcc,
                  ((0, 0), (0, self.n_frames - mfcc.shape[1]))
              )
          return mfcc  # shape (13, 40)

      def load_dataset_from_folders(self, data_dir, label_map):
          """Load all audio files from labelled subdirectories."""
          X, y = [], []
          for label_name, label_idx in label_map.items():
              folder = os.path.join(data_dir, label_name)
              for fname in os.listdir(folder):
                  fpath = os.path.join(folder, fname)
                  try:
                      audio = self.load_audio(fpath)
                      mfcc  = self.extract_mfcc(audio)
                      X.append(mfcc)
                      y.append(label_idx)
                  except Exception as e:
                      print(f"Skipped {fpath}: {e}")
          return np.array(X), np.array(y)

      def normalise(self, X_train, X_val, X_test):
          """Per-coefficient z-score normalisation."""
          # X shape: (N, 13, 40)
          mean = X_train.mean(axis=(0, 2), keepdims=True)  # (1,13,1)
          std  = X_train.std(axis=(0, 2), keepdims=True)   # (1,13,1)
          std[std == 0] = 1e-8  # avoid division by zero
          return ((X_train-mean)/std,
                  (X_val  -mean)/std,
                  (X_test -mean)/std,
                  mean.squeeze(), std.squeeze())


APPENDIX B — CNN MODEL ARCHITECTURE SOURCE CODE
-------------------------------------------------

The following is the SoundClassifierCNN implementation in TensorFlow/Keras:

  def build_model(input_shape=(13, 40, 1), n_classes=7):
      inputs = keras.Input(shape=input_shape)

      # Block 1 — 32 filters
      x = layers.Conv2D(32, (3,3), activation='relu', padding='same')(inputs)
      x = layers.BatchNormalization()(x)
      x = layers.MaxPooling2D((2,2))(x)
      x = layers.Dropout(0.25)(x)

      # Block 2 — 64 filters
      x = layers.Conv2D(64, (3,3), activation='relu', padding='same')(x)
      x = layers.BatchNormalization()(x)
      x = layers.MaxPooling2D((2,2))(x)
      x = layers.Dropout(0.25)(x)

      # Block 3 — 128 filters
      x = layers.Conv2D(128, (3,3), activation='relu', padding='same')(x)
      x = layers.BatchNormalization()(x)
      x = layers.MaxPooling2D((2,2))(x)
      x = layers.Dropout(0.30)(x)

      x = layers.Flatten()(x)

      # Dense Block 1
      x = layers.Dense(256, activation='relu')(x)
      x = layers.BatchNormalization()(x)
      x = layers.Dropout(0.50)(x)

      # Dense Block 2
      x = layers.Dense(128, activation='relu')(x)
      x = layers.BatchNormalization()(x)
      x = layers.Dropout(0.40)(x)

      # Output
      outputs = layers.Dense(n_classes, activation='softmax')(x)

      model = keras.Model(inputs, outputs)
      model.compile(
          optimizer=keras.optimizers.Adam(learning_rate=0.001),
          loss='sparse_categorical_crossentropy',
          metrics=['accuracy']
      )
      return model

  # Training with callbacks
  callbacks = [
      keras.callbacks.EarlyStopping(
          monitor='val_loss', patience=15,
          restore_best_weights=True
      ),
      keras.callbacks.ModelCheckpoint(
          filepath='models/best_model.keras',
          monitor='val_accuracy', save_best_only=True
      ),
      keras.callbacks.ReduceLROnPlateau(
          monitor='val_loss', factor=0.5,
          patience=5, min_lr=1e-7
      )
  ]

  history = model.fit(
      X_train, y_train,
      validation_data=(X_val, y_val),
      epochs=100,
      batch_size=32,
      class_weight=class_weights,
      callbacks=callbacks
  )


APPENDIX C — FLASK API SERVER SOURCE CODE
-------------------------------------------

Core structure of the Flask inference API:

  from flask import Flask, request, jsonify
  from flask_cors import CORS
  import tensorflow as tf
  import librosa, soundfile, numpy as np, json, io

  app = Flask(__name__)
  CORS(app)

  model = None
  metadata = None

  def load_model_once():
      global model, metadata
      if model is None:
          model = tf.keras.models.load_model('models/best_model.keras')
          with open('data/processed/alert_sounds/metadata.json') as f:
              metadata = json.load(f)

  def preprocess_audio(audio_bytes):
      """Decode audio bytes, extract MFCC, normalise."""
      try:
          audio, sr = soundfile.read(io.BytesIO(audio_bytes))
      except Exception:
          audio, sr = librosa.load(io.BytesIO(audio_bytes), sr=None)

      audio = librosa.resample(audio, orig_sr=sr, target_sr=22050)
      target = int(22050 * 2.5)
      if len(audio) < target:
          audio = np.pad(audio, (0, target - len(audio)))
      else:
          audio = audio[:target]

      trimmed, _ = librosa.effects.trim(audio, top_db=40)
      if len(trimmed) >= int(22050 * 0.3):
          audio = np.pad(trimmed, (0, max(0, target - len(trimmed))))[:target]

      mfcc = librosa.feature.mfcc(y=audio, sr=22050, n_mfcc=13)
      if mfcc.shape[1] > 40:
          mfcc = mfcc[:, :40]
      else:
          mfcc = np.pad(mfcc, ((0,0),(0, 40-mfcc.shape[1])))

      mean = np.array(metadata['mfcc_mean'])
      std  = np.array(metadata['mfcc_std'])
      mfcc = (mfcc - mean[:, None]) / std[:, None]
      return mfcc.reshape(1, 13, 40, 1).astype(np.float32)

  CLASS_MAP = {
      'bus horns':  ('bus-horn',   '🚌', 'high'),
      'car horns':  ('car-horn',   '🚗', 'medium'),
      'train horns':('train-horn', '🚂', 'high'),
      'truck horns':('truck-horn', '🚚', 'high'),
      'ambulance':  ('ambulance',  '🚑', 'high'),
      'firetruck':  ('firetruck',  '🚒', 'high'),
      'police':     ('police',     '🚓', 'high'),
  }

  @app.route('/predict', methods=['POST'])
  def predict():
      load_model_once()
      audio_file = request.files.get('audio')
      if not audio_file:
          return jsonify({'error': 'No audio file provided'}), 400

      features = preprocess_audio(audio_file.read())
      probs = model.predict(features, verbose=0)[0]
      idx   = int(np.argmax(probs))
      conf  = float(probs[idx])
      label = metadata['class_names'][idx]
      alert_type, icon, severity = CLASS_MAP.get(
          label, (label, '🔊', 'low')
      )
      return jsonify({
          'id': str(uuid.uuid4()),
          'type': alert_type,
          'title': label,
          'icon': icon,
          'severity': severity,
          'confidence': round(conf * 100, 1),
          'timestamp': datetime.utcnow().isoformat(),
          'detected': conf >= 0.60
      })

  @app.route('/health', methods=['GET'])
  def health():
      return jsonify({'status': 'ok', 'model_loaded': model is not None})

  if __name__ == '__main__':
      app.run(host='0.0.0.0', port=5003, debug=False)
