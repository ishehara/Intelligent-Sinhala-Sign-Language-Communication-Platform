ABSTRACT
--------
Sign language recognition systems that rely solely on hand gestures fail to
capture the full communicative intent of the signer, because sign languages
also encode meaning through facial expressions and body posture. This paper
presents a real-time Sinhala Sign Language (SSL) recognition system that
simultaneously analyses three modalities — hand gestures, facial expressions,
and body posture — through a purpose-built Multi-Stream Fusion Model. The
system uses Google MediaPipe to extract 457-dimensional skeletal features per
video frame, covering 42 hand landmarks, 60 key facial landmarks with 52
blendshape expression scores, and 33 body pose landmarks. Each modality is
processed by a dedicated sub-network: Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs)
for hands and face, and a Bidirectional LSTM for body posture. An attention-
based fusion module dynamically learns the relative importance of each stream
per sign and combines them into a unified prediction. Four architectures were
systematically explored — Multimodal LSTM (~47% val acc), Multimodal
Transformer (~41%), Hybrid LSTM-Transformer (~52%), and the final Multi-Stream
Fusion Model (76.3% val acc, 80.1% test acc on 383 classes). The model was
trained on 8,472 videos across 383 Sinhala sign vocabulary items under severe
data scarcity (~22 videos per class), addressed through skeleton-level
augmentation, label smoothing, and aggressive regularisation. The system
performs real-time inference from a live camera feed and is deployed through a
Flask REST API for integration with a React Native mobile application, forming
one component of a broader platform aimed at improving communication
accessibility for the deaf community in Sri Lanka.


1. INTRODUCTION
---------------

1.1 Background
Sign language is the primary mode of communication for deaf and hearing-
impaired communities. In Sri Lanka, Sinhala Sign Language (SSL) is used by an
estimated 200,000 deaf individuals. However, the overwhelming majority of the
hearing population does not know SSL, creating a persistent communication
barrier in everyday life — in hospitals, classrooms, workplaces, government
offices, and on the street.

Human interpreters are scarce and expensive, and are unavailable for spontaneous
everyday interactions. An automated system that can observe a person performing
SSL through a standard camera and produce the corresponding text in real time
would significantly reduce this barrier, enabling deaf individuals to
communicate independently with any hearing person who has access to a
smartphone.

1.2 Problem Statement
Existing sign language recognition systems overwhelmingly focus on hand gesture
classification alone. While the hands are the primary articulators in sign
language, they are not the only carriers of meaning. In SSL and most sign
languages worldwide, facial expression and body posture serve critical
linguistic functions:

  - Facial expressions distinguish grammatical constructions (questions versus
    statements often differ only in eyebrow position), convey emotional tone,
    and in some signs are themselves part of the sign's definition.
  - Body posture provides context such as emphasis (leaning forward) or
    referential direction (body orientation indicating the subject or object
    of a sentence).

A hand-only system misses these signals and is therefore limited in accuracy
and communicative completeness. A system that integrates all three modalities
— hands, face, and body — mirrors how human interpreters read sign language
and should achieve higher recognition accuracy.

1.3 Objectives
This work addresses the following objectives:

  (1)  Design a feature extraction pipeline that captures hand gestures, facial
       expressions (including expression intensity through blendshape scores),
       and body posture from video input using state-of-the-art pose estimation.

  (2)  Develop a multi-stream neural network architecture where each modality
       is processed by a specialised sub-network matched to its temporal
       dynamics, with attention-based fusion that learns the relative
       contribution of each modality per sign.

  (3)  Address the practical challenge of severe data scarcity (~22 training
       videos per class across 383 classes) through targeted augmentation and
       regularisation strategies.

  (4)  Deploy the trained model as a real-time inference system accessible to
       a mobile application.

2. RELATED WORK
---------------

2.1 Hand-Only Sign Language Recognition
Most published sign language recognition systems treat the problem as hand
gesture classification. Early work used hand-crafted features such as HOG
descriptors and histogram-based skin detection, fed into classifiers such as
SVMs or Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) [1]. Deep learning approaches improved
accuracy substantially: CNNs for static fingerspelling [2], LSTMs for
temporal gesture sequences [3], and Transformer architectures for capturing
long-range dependencies in continuous signing [4].

However, these approaches capture only one dimension of sign language. They
may achieve high accuracy on constrained datasets of isolated hand gestures but
struggle with naturalistic signing where meaning depends on simultaneous
signals across the body.

2.2 Multi-Modal Sign Language Recognition
A growing body of work has explored multi-modal approaches. Koller et al. [5]
combined CNN-based hand features with mouth shape features for German Sign
Language, showing that lip movements improved recognition of mouth-action
signs. Camgoz et al. [6] explored joint modelling of hand shape, hand motion,
and mouth pattern for continuous SLR. More recently, Jiang et al. [7]
demonstrated that skeleton-based multi-stream architectures (hands + body)
outperformed single-stream baselines on Chinese Sign Language.

These works validate the importance of multi-modal features but typically
focus on two modalities (hands + mouth, or hands + body). This work extends
the approach to three full modalities — hands, face, and body — and introduces
an attention fusion mechanism that provides interpretable stream-level
importance weights.

2.3 MediaPipe for Pose Estimation
Google MediaPipe [8] provides real-time, on-device pose estimation models for
hands (21 landmarks per hand), face (468 landmarks + 52 blendshapes), and
body pose (33 landmarks). MediaPipe has been widely adopted for sign language
recognition research due to its accuracy, speed, and cross-platform
availability. This work uses the MediaPipe Tasks API (v0.10.21) as the
feature extraction backbone.

2.4 Sign Language Recognition in Sri Lanka
Research on SSL recognition is sparse. Existing work has been limited to small
vocabulary sizes (typically 10–50 signs) and hand-only approaches using
conventional machine learning [9]. No published system integrates facial
expression analysis or body posture for SSL, and none addresses a vocabulary
as large as 383 signs. This work represents a significant scale-up for SSL
recognition research.

2.5 Temporal Convolutional Networks
TCNs [10] use causal dilated convolutions to capture temporal patterns at
multiple scales efficiently. Compared to RNNs, TCNs offer parallelisable
training, stable gradients, and flexible receptive field control through
dilation. They have shown competitive or superior performance to LSTMs on
sequence-modelling tasks including action recognition and speech processing.


3. DATASET
----------

3.1 Source
The dataset used is the signVideo dataset, a locally collected video corpus of
Sinhala Sign Language. Each sign class is stored as a directory containing
multiple MP4 video recordings of a signer performing the corresponding sign.
Videos vary in frame rate and resolution but depict a single signer against
a relatively uniform background.

3.2 Vocabulary and Statistics
  Total classes          : 383 Sinhala sign vocabulary items
  Total videos           : 8,472
  Average per class      : approximately 22 videos
  Minimum per class      : varies (some classes have as few as 15 videos)

The 383 classes span a broad communicative vocabulary:
  - Greetings and social phrases (e.g., hello, thank you, goodbye)
  - People and relationships (e.g., mother, father, teacher, friend)
  - Numbers (cardinal and ordinal)
  - Days of the week and months of the year
  - Colours and descriptive adjectives
  - Common nouns (objects, places, animals)
  - Verbs and actions (e.g., eat, go, walk, help)
  - Prepositions, conjunctions, determiners
  - Adverbs and interjections

This coverage enables the system to interpret a broad range of conversational
utterances.

3.3 Dataset Split
A stratified split ensured proportional class representation:
  Training   : 5,827 videos (68.8%)
  Validation : 1,358 videos (16.0%)
  Test       : 1,287 videos (15.2%)

Label mappings were stored in a centralised label_mapping.json file mapping
each Sinhala sign name to a zero-based integer index.

3.4 Data Scarcity
With approximately 22 videos per class, the dataset is severely under-resourced
by deep learning standards. This poses a critical risk: the model has strong
incentive to memorise training samples rather than learning generalisable
representations. Addressing this challenge was a central design concern
(Sections 5 and 8).


4. FEATURE EXTRACTION AND NORMALISATION
-----------------------------------------

4.1 MediaPipe Feature Extraction
Three pre-trained MediaPipe task models were deployed:
  - Hand Landmarker     (float16, GPU delegate)
  - Face Landmarker     (float16, GPU delegate, blendshapes enabled)
  - Pose Landmarker     (full model, float16)

Each video was decoded frame-by-frame using OpenCV, converted from BGR to RGB,
and passed to the three detectors. A fixed temporal window of 60 frames was
used: shorter videos were zero-padded; longer videos were uniformly subsampled.

4.2 Three Feature Streams
Three distinct feature streams were extracted per frame:

  (a) Hand Stream — 126 dimensions
      Both hands tracked simultaneously. Each hand provides 21 landmarks with
      (x, y, z) normalised coordinates: 21 × 3 = 63 per hand, 126 total.
      Undetected hands were represented as zero vectors. Detection thresholds
      were set to 0.3 for robust tracking of fast-moving and partially
      occluded hands.

  (b) Face Stream — 232 dimensions
      From MediaPipe's full 468 face landmarks, 60 key landmarks were selected
      based on their linguistic and emotional relevance:
        - 12 eye landmarks (gaze direction, eye openness)
        - 10 eyebrow landmarks (raised, furrowed expressions)
        - 14 mouth landmarks (lip shape, speech-related movements)
        - 4 nose landmarks (spatial reference point)
        - 20 face oval landmarks (structural context)
      These 60 landmarks provide 60 × 3 = 180 spatial dimensions. Additionally,
      52 MediaPipe face blendshape scores were included. Blendshapes are
      scalar values (0–1) representing muscle-group-level expression
      coefficients such as jaw_open, brow_inner_up, cheek_puff, and
      mouth_smile_left. They capture expression intensity information that
      spatial landmark positions alone cannot fully represent. Total face
      features: 180 + 52 = 232 dimensions.

  (c) Pose Stream — 99 dimensions
      All 33 MediaPipe body landmarks extracted as (x, y, z) coordinates:
      33 × 3 = 99 dimensions. These capture shoulder orientation, torso
      lean, arm elevation, and upper-body posture.

4.3 Combined Feature Vector
The three streams were concatenated in fixed order per frame:
  [hands | face | pose] = [126 | 232 | 99] = 457 dimensions
Each video was represented as a tensor of shape (60 × 457).

4.4 Root-Relative Coordinate Normalisation
Raw MediaPipe coordinates (0–1 normalised to image frame) are sensitive to
the signer's position and distance from the camera. To achieve position
invariance, root-relative normalisation was applied:

  - Hands:  Each hand's 21 landmarks centred on the wrist (landmark 0).
  - Face:   60 landmarks centred on the nose tip (landmark 36 in the
            filtered set). Blendshape scores (scalars) were unmodified.
  - Pose:   33 landmarks centred on the mid-shoulder point (average of
            landmarks 11 and 12).

Undetected body parts (all-zero blocks) remained zero after centring. This
normalisation was applied identically in training and inference pipelines.


5. DATA AUGMENTATION
---------------------
Skeleton-level augmentation was applied during training to combat data scarcity.
Augmentation operated on raw coordinates before normalisation to preserve
geometric consistency. A stream-specific strategy applied targeted
perturbations appropriate to each modality's content.

Seven stochastic operations were used (global application probability 0.7):

  Operation          Parameters                   Purpose
  ------------------ ---------------------------- ----------------------------------
  Spatial rotation   ±5° on (x,y) pairs           Camera angle variation
  Spatial scaling    factor ∈ [0.9, 1.1]           Distance variation
  Gaussian noise     σ = 0.002                     Sensor noise robustness
  Temporal shift     ±10% frames, p=0.3            Signing onset variation
  Time warping       speed ∈ [0.8, 1.2], p=0.25   Signing rate variation
  Frame dropping     up to 20%, p=0.2              Occlusion simulation
  Time masking       contiguous block, p=0.15      Brief invisibility

Horizontal flipping was deliberately excluded because in sign language, left-
right handedness carries semantic meaning; mirroring a sign can change its
meaning. Augmentation was disabled for validation and test sets.


6. MODEL ARCHITECTURE EXPLORATION
-----------------------------------
Four architectures were systematically evaluated, with each iteration
addressing limitations observed in its predecessor.

  Architecture                      Params    Val Acc    Limitation
  --------------------------------- --------- --------- ---------------------------
  Model 1: Multimodal BiLSTM        ~3.1M     ~47%      Single stream conflates
            (2-layer, hidden=256,                        heterogeneous features
             attention pooling)

  Model 2: Multimodal Transformer   ~4.7M     ~41%      Data-hungry; no benefit
            (4 layers, d=256,                            over LSTM at this scale
             8 heads, GELU)

  Model 3: Hybrid LSTM+Transformer  ~5.2M     ~52%      Still single stream;
            (BiLSTM → 2-layer                            cannot specialise per
             Transformer)                                modality

  Model 4: Multi-Stream Fusion      ~2.52M    76.3%     FINAL — separate streams
            (TCN + BiLSTM + Attn)                        resolve conflation

Key finding: all three single-stream architectures (Models 1–3) suffered from
conflating hand dynamics, facial expression changes, and body posture shifts
into one undifferentiated input. Despite increasing architectural complexity,
they plateaued at ~52% because the network could not learn modality-specific
temporal patterns. Separating the streams (Model 4) produced a 24-point
improvement while using fewer parameters.


7. FINAL MODEL — MULTI-STREAM FUSION MODEL
--------------------------------------------

7.1 Design Rationale
The three modalities have fundamentally different temporal dynamics:
  - Hands: rapid, high-frequency movements (sub-second transitions)
  - Face: moderate-speed, continuous expression changes
  - Body: slow, contextual posture shifts over the full sign duration

Routing each modality through a dedicated sub-network allows the architecture
to match each signal's characteristics. The TCN was chosen for hands and face
because dilated convolutions efficiently capture multi-scale temporal patterns.
The BiLSTM was chosen for body posture because its sequential gating mechanism
maintains long-range context for slow-changing signals.

7.2 Architecture

  Input: (batch, 60 frames, 457 features)
  ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │                                                                      │
  │  Hand Stream (dims 0–125)    Face Stream (dims 126–357)              │
  │  ┌──────────────────────┐    ┌──────────────────────────┐            │
  │  │ TCN Block 1:         │    │ TCN Block 1:             │            │
  │  │   Conv1D 126→64      │    │   Conv1D 232→128         │            │
  │  │   dilation=1, k=5    │    │   dilation=1, k=5        │            │
  │  │   BN + ReLU + Drop   │    │   BN + ReLU + Drop       │            │
  │  │   Residual (1×1 conv)│    │   Residual (1×1 conv)    │            │
  │  ├──────────────────────┤    ├──────────────────────────┤            │
  │  │ TCN Block 2:         │    │ TCN Block 2:             │            │
  │  │   Conv1D 64→128      │    │   Conv1D 128→256         │            │
  │  │   dilation=2, k=5    │    │   dilation=2, k=5        │            │
  │  ├──────────────────────┤    ├──────────────────────────┤            │
  │  │ TCN Block 3:         │    │ TCN Block 3:             │            │
  │  │   Conv1D 128→128     │    │   Conv1D 256→256         │            │
  │  │   dilation=4, k=5    │    │   dilation=4, k=5        │            │
  │  ├──────────────────────┤    ├──────────────────────────┤            │
  │  │ Global Avg Pool      │    │ Global Avg Pool          │            │
  │  └────────┬─────────────┘    └────────┬─────────────────┘            │
  │           │ (128-d)                   │ (256-d)                      │
  │           │                           │                              │
  │  Pose Stream (dims 358–456)           │                              │
  │  ┌──────────────────────┐             │                              │
  │  │ BiLSTM               │             │                              │
  │  │   input=99, h=32×2   │             │                              │
  │  │   1 layer, bidir     │             │                              │
  │  │ Adaptive Avg Pool    │             │                              │
  │  └────────┬─────────────┘             │                              │
  │           │ (64-d)                    │                              │
  │           │                           │                              │
  │  ┌────────┴───────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┐   │
  │  │                  ATTENTION FUSION                              │   │
  │  │  Project each stream → 512-d                                  │   │
  │  │  Score: Linear(512→256) → Tanh → Linear(256→1) per stream     │   │
  │  │  Softmax over 3 scores → weights (w_hand, w_face, w_pose)     │   │
  │  │  Fused = w_hand·hand + w_face·face + w_pose·pose              │   │
  │  │  LayerNorm → Dropout(0.3) →  512-d fused vector               │   │
  │  └──────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────┘   │
  │                             │                                        │
  │  ┌──────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────────┐   │
  │  │                     CLASSIFIER                                │   │
  │  │  Linear(512→256) → ReLU → Dropout(0.3) → Linear(256→383)     │   │
  │  └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
  │                                                                      │
  │  Output: logits (batch, 383)  +  attention_weights (batch, 3)        │
  └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  Total trainable parameters: ~2.52 million

7.3 Stream Details

  Hand TCN Stream
  Three TCN blocks with exponentially increasing dilation (1, 2, 4) and
  kernel size 5. Each block contains two 1D convolutions with BatchNorm,
  ReLU, and dropout (0.3), plus a residual skip connection (1×1 convolution
  when dimensions change). Channel progression: 126 → 64 → 128 → 128.
  Global average pooling over the time axis produces a 128-d vector
  representing the full hand motion trajectory.

  Face TCN Stream
  Same architecture with wider channels to match the richer face input:
  232 → 128 → 256 → 256. Produces a 256-d vector capturing expression
  dynamics across the sign.

  Pose BiLSTM Stream
  A single-layer bidirectional LSTM (hidden size 32 per direction, 64 total).
  Adaptive average pooling over the output sequence produces a 64-d vector
  representing body posture context.

7.4 Attention Fusion
Each stream's output is projected to a common 512-d space. A shared two-layer
attention network computes a scalar score per stream; softmax normalisation
produces three weights summing to 1. The fused vector is the weighted sum
followed by LayerNorm and Dropout.

The attention weights provide interpretability: they reveal whether the model
relies more on hands, face, or body for each individual sign, providing
insight into the model's decision process.


8. TRAINING PROCEDURE
-----------------------

  Parameter                  Value
  -------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Loss function              Cross-entropy + label smoothing (ε=0.1)
  Optimiser                  Adam (lr=1×10⁻³, weight decay=1×10⁻⁴)
  LR schedule                CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts
                             (T₀=30, T_mult=2, η_min=1×10⁻⁶)
  Batch size                 16
  Max epochs                 120
  Early stopping patience    20 epochs (monitor: val accuracy)
  Dropout                    0.3 (all sub-networks + classifier)
  Augmentation               Skeleton-level, 7 ops, p=0.7
  Hardware                   NVIDIA GeForce MX350
  Framework                  PyTorch 2.7.1 + CUDA 11.8

Label smoothing was used because the extreme data scarcity (~22 samples per
class) makes the model prone to overconfident predictions on memorised
training data. By distributing ε = 0.1 of the probability mass across
non-target classes, the training signal encourages broader generalisation.

Cosine Annealing with Warm Restarts was chosen over ReduceLROnPlateau because
the periodic resetting of the learning rate enables the optimiser to escape
local minima that plateau-based schedulers cannot, while the progressively
longer cycles (30 → 60 → 120 epochs) give the model more time to refine in
later stages.


9. EVALUATION RESULTS
-----------------------

9.1 Final Model Performance

  Metric                         Value
  ------------------------------ ------
  Test accuracy                  80.1%
  Validation accuracy (best)     76.3%
  Best checkpoint epoch          87
  Classes                        383
  Training videos                5,827
  Test videos                    1,287
  Model parameters               ~2.52M

9.2 Architecture Comparison

  Architecture                       Val Acc   Test Acc   Params
  ---------------------------------- --------- --------- --------
  Multimodal BiLSTM                  ~47%      —         ~3.1M
  Multimodal Transformer             ~41%      —         ~4.7M
  Hybrid LSTM + Transformer          ~52%      —         ~5.2M
  Multi-Stream Fusion (final)        76.3%     80.1%     ~2.52M

The multi-stream approach improved validation accuracy by approximately 24
percentage points over the best single-stream baseline (Hybrid, ~52%) while
using fewer parameters (2.52M vs 5.2M). This demonstrates that architectural
design — specifically, modality-specific processing before fusion — is more
effective than simply increasing model size when the training data is limited.

9.3 Impact of Multi-Modal Features
The progression from Model 1 to Model 4 isolates the contribution of the
multi-stream design. Models 1–3 all received the same 457-dimensional input
containing all three modalities but processed them as a single concatenated
stream. Their accuracy plateaued around 47–52%. Model 4 received identical
input but split it into three dedicated streams with targeted architectures.
The 24-point improvement confirms that the feature separation — not the
features themselves — is the critical factor, validating the hypothesis that
hand dynamics, facial expressions, and body posture require modality-specific
temporal modelling.

9.4 Discussion of Test vs Validation Gap
The test accuracy (80.1%) exceeds validation accuracy (76.3%) by 3.8 points.
This is consistent with natural variance at this dataset scale (~1,287 test
and ~1,358 validation samples) and does not indicate data leakage or
overfitting to the test set: the best checkpoint was selected exclusively
based on validation performance, and the test set was evaluated only once.


10. SYSTEM DEPLOYMENT
-----------------------

10.1 Real-Time Inference Pipeline
The trained model is deployed for real-time sign recognition through the
following pipeline:

  1. Camera captures frames at native frame rate using OpenCV VideoCapture.
  2. Each frame is passed through MediaPipe hand, face, and pose detectors.
  3. The extracted 457-dimensional feature vector is root-relative normalised
     using the same procedure applied during training.
  4. Features are accumulated in a rolling 60-frame buffer.
  5. When the buffer is full, the model performs a single forward pass.
  6. The predicted sign (Sinhala text) and confidence score are displayed
     on-screen using Pillow (PIL) with the Nirmala UI font, since OpenCV
     does not support Sinhala Unicode rendering.

10.2 Backend API
A Flask REST API (react_native_bridge.py, port 5000) exposes the inference
pipeline over HTTP. The React Native mobile application sends camera frame
data to the server and receives the predicted sign, enabling server-side
inference without requiring the deep learning model to run on the mobile
device.

Key API endpoints:
  POST /predict-sign       : Single-frame prediction
  POST /predict-video-sign : Multi-frame video prediction
  POST /clear-video-buffer : Reset the rolling buffer
  GET  /all-labels         : Retrieve all available sign labels

10.3 Sinhala Unicode Rendering
A specific technical challenge was rendering Sinhala script on the inference
display. OpenCV's putText function only supports ASCII characters. This was
solved by converting the OpenCV frame (BGR) to a PIL Image, using PIL's text
rendering with the Nirmala UI font (Nirmala.ttc — a Unicode-capable system
font), and converting back to OpenCV format. This approach supports the full
Sinhala Unicode range without external font dependencies.


11. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
---------------------------------

11.1 Dataset Limitations
  - Single signer: The dataset is recorded with a limited number of signers.
    Models trained on single-signer data may not generalise well to signers
    with different physical characteristics, signing styles, or speeds.
    Multi-signer training data would improve robustness.
  - Data scarcity: ~22 videos per class is far below the thousands of examples
    per-class typical in state-of-the-art recognition systems. Transfer
    learning from large-scale sign language datasets in other languages could
    partially address this.
  - Vocabulary coverage: 383 classes cover a useful conversational subset but
    not the full SSL vocabulary. Expanding the vocabulary would require
    additional data collection.

11.2 Model Limitations
  - Isolated sign recognition: The current system recognises individual
    signs rather than continuous signing sequences. Connectionist Temporal
    Classification (CTC) or encoder-decoder architectures would be needed
    for continuous recognition.
  - 60-frame fixed window: Signs that span more or fewer than 60 frames may
    lose information through truncation or zero-padding. Dynamic sequence
    length handling would improve flexibility.
  - No language model: Predictions are made independently per sign without
    consideration of linguistic context. Integrating a language model could
    improve accuracy by resolving ambiguities using the probability of sign
    sequences in SSL grammar.

11.3 Deployment Limitations
  - Server dependency: The current deployment requires a server running the
    Flask API. Converting the PyTorch model to ONNX or TFLite for on-device
    inference would eliminate the server requirement and reduce latency.
  - Latency: The rolling 60-frame buffer introduces an inherent latency equal
    to the time required to capture 60 frames. At 30 fps, this is 2 seconds.
    A sliding window with overlapping predictions would provide earlier
    preliminary results.

11.4 Future Work
  - Continuous sign language recognition using CTC or sequence-to-sequence
    models.
  - Multi-signer training with data from diverse signers.
  - On-device deployment via PyTorch Mobile or TFLite conversion.
  - Transfer learning from larger sign language datasets (e.g., WLASL, MSASL)
    to address data scarcity.
  - Language model integration for sequence-level disambiguation.
  - Achieving 80%+ validation accuracy through expanded multi-signer
    training data, transfer learning from larger sign language corpora
    (e.g., WLASL, MSASL), and hyperparameter optimisation — the current
    76.3% validation accuracy demonstrates the viability of the multi-stream
    approach, and surpassing the 80% threshold is an attainable near-term
    goal with additional data and fine-tuning.
  - User studies with the deaf community to evaluate real-world usability.


12. CONCLUSION
--------------
This paper presented a multi-stream fusion approach to Sinhala Sign Language
recognition that simultaneously analyses hand gestures, facial expressions,
and body posture to produce more accurate recognition than hand-only systems.

The key contribution is the Multi-Stream Fusion Model, which routes each
modality through a dedicated temporal sub-network — TCNs for the rapid
dynamics of hand and face, BiLSTM for the slow context of body posture — and
combines them through an attention-based fusion mechanism that dynamically
learns each modality's importance per sign.

Systematic evaluation across four architectures demonstrated that modality-
specific processing before fusion is critical: the multi-stream design achieved
76.3% validation accuracy and 80.1% test accuracy on 383 sign classes,
improving over the best single-stream architecture by 24 percentage points
while using fewer parameters. This was accomplished under severe data scarcity
(~22 videos per class) using targeted skeleton-level augmentation, label
smoothing, and aggressive regularisation.

The system operates in real time from a live camera feed and is deployed as a
Flask REST API for integration with a React Native mobile application. As part
of the Intelligent Sinhala Sign Language Communication Platform, it
complements text-to-sign generation, environmental sound alerts, and adaptive
learning modules to provide comprehensive communication and safety tools for
the deaf community in Sri Lanka.
