                              ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Sign language recognition systems that rely exclusively on hand gesture
analysis fail to capture the full communicative intent of a signer because
sign languages encode meaning simultaneously through hand shapes, facial
expressions, and body posture. This research presents a real-time Sinhala
Sign Language (SSL) recognition system — referred to as the SSL Reader — that
addresses this limitation through a purpose-built Multi-Stream Fusion Model
that analyses all three modalities concurrently.

The system employs Google MediaPipe Tasks API (v0.10.21) to extract a
457-dimensional feature vector from each video frame, combining 126 dimensions
of hand landmark coordinates (both hands, 21 landmarks each), 232 dimensions of
facial features (60 key landmarks plus 52 expression blendshape scores), and
99 dimensions of full body pose landmarks (33 joints). Each modality is
processed by a dedicated sub-network whose architecture is matched to the
temporal characteristics of that modality: Temporal Convolutional Networks
(TCNs) with exponentially dilated convolutions handle the rapid dynamics of
hand gestures and the continuous changes in facial expression; a Bidirectional
Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) network handles the slower, context-dependent
changes in body posture. An attention-based fusion module learns to
dynamically weight the three streams for each individual sign before a
two-layer classifier produces the final prediction.

Four model architectures were systematically developed and evaluated:
Multimodal BiLSTM (~47% validation accuracy), Multimodal Transformer (~41%),
Hybrid LSTM-Transformer (~52%), and the final Multi-Stream Fusion Model (76.3%
validation accuracy, 80.1% test accuracy on 383 Sinhala sign classes). The
30-percentage-point improvement over the best single-stream architecture
demonstrates that modality-specific processing before fusion is the critical
design decision.

The model was trained on 8,472 videos spanning 383 Sinhala sign vocabulary
items under severe data scarcity (~22 videos per class), addressed through
skeleton-level augmentation (seven stochastic operations), label smoothing,
and dropout regularisation. The trained model is deployed as a Flask REST API
that streams predictions to a React Native mobile application, forming the
recognition module of the broader Intelligent Sinhala Sign Language
Communication Platform.

Keywords: Sinhala Sign Language, Multi-Stream Fusion, Temporal Convolutional
Network, BiLSTM, MediaPipe, Attention Fusion, Multi-Modal Recognition,
Real-Time Inference

                    CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
================================================================================
================================================================================

Sign language is the primary and natural language of millions of deaf and
hearing-impaired people around the world. Unlike spoken languages, which rely
on the auditory-vocal channel, sign languages are visual-spatial languages that
transmit meaning through coordinated hand shapes, hand movements, facial
expressions, and body posture. Sign languages are full, natural languages with
their own grammar, syntax, and vocabulary — they are not simplified codes or
derivatives of spoken languages.

In Sri Lanka, the deaf and hearing-impaired community comprises an estimated
200,000 individuals who communicate primarily through Sinhala Sign Language
(SSL). Despite this substantial population, SSL remains largely unknown to the
general hearing public, creating a pervasive communication barrier that affects
nearly every aspect of daily life. A deaf individual visiting a hospital, a
school, a government office, or a shop confronts the same barrier: there is no
shared language between them and the hearing person they need to communicate
with. Professional sign language interpreters exist but are scarce and expensive,
and are unavailable for the spontaneous, informal interactions of everyday life.

Automated sign language recognition — using computer vision and machine learning
to interpret sign language from video input — offers a technological approach to
breaking down this barrier. If a smartphone or computer can recognise what a
person is signing in real time and produce the corresponding text, it becomes
possible for deaf individuals to communicate independently with hearing people
who have access to that device.

This thesis presents the design, implementation, and evaluation of the SSL
Reader component: a deep learning system that performs real-time Sinhala Sign
Language recognition using a novel multi-modal approach. While the majority of
published sign language recognition systems analyse only hand gestures, the SSL
Reader simultaneously analyses hand gestures, facial expressions, and body
posture. This three-modality approach mirrors how human interpreters read sign
language and produces significantly more accurate recognition than hand-only
systems.

The SSL Reader is developed as one component of a larger platform — the
Intelligent Sinhala Sign Language Communication Platform (ISSLCP) — that also
includes text-to-sign animation, environmental sound alerting, and adaptive
learning modules. Together, these components aim to address communication
accessibility and safety needs of the deaf community in Sri Lanka.

This chapter reviews the background literature, identifies the research gap,
states the research problem, and defines the research objectives. Subsequent
chapters describe the methodology (Chapter 2), present and discuss the results
(Chapter 3), and draw conclusions (Chapter 4).


1.1 BACKGROUND LITERATURE
--------------------------

1.1.1 Sign Language Recognition: An Overview

The field of automatic sign language recognition (SLR) has been studied for
over three decades, beginning with early systems in the 1990s that recognised
small vocabularies of static hand postures (Liddell and Johnson, 1989). The
fundamental challenge in SLR is significantly greater than in many other
pattern recognition domains: signs are not static postures but dynamic temporal
sequences, signers vary widely in their signing style, speed, and body
proportions, and the visual appearance of a sign depends heavily on lighting,
background, and camera angle.

Sign languages can be analysed at several levels. At the phonological level,
signs are composed of simultaneous parameters including handshape (the
configuration of the fingers and thumb), location (where in signing space the
hand is positioned), movement (the trajectory the hand follows), orientation
(the direction the palm and fingers face), and non-manual features (facial
expressions, head tilt, mouth movements, and body posture). This simultaneous
multi-channel structure is what makes sign language recognition fundamentally
different from handwriting recognition or gesture spotting in isolation.

The problem is typically framed as: given a video sequence of a person signing,
classify the sequence as one of N known signs. Three main sub-tasks are
recognised in the literature:
  (a) Isolated sign recognition — classify individual, segmented signs
  (b) Sign spotting — detect sign boundaries within continuous video
  (c) Continuous sign language recognition — recognise full sentences

This research addresses isolated sign recognition, which is a necessary
precondition for the more challenging continuous recognition task.

1.1.2 Traditional Machine Learning Approaches

Early SLR systems relied on hand-engineered features extracted from video
frames, fed into classical machine learning classifiers. Common feature
engineering approaches included:

  - Skin colour segmentation to isolate hand regions from the image background
  - Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) descriptors to capture hand shape
  - Optical flow fields to represent hand motion between frames
  - Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) coefficients for frequency-domain features
  - Depth map analysis from RGB-D cameras (Kinect sensors) that provided
    explicit 3D information not available from standard cameras

These features were fed into classifiers including:
  - Support Vector Machines (SVMs), which showed strong performance on small,
    well-engineered feature sets (Fang et al., 2007)
  - Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), which explicitly model temporal sequences and
    became the dominant approach for dynamic gesture recognition through the
    2000s (Starner and Pentland, 1995)
  - k-Nearest Neighbours classifiers for simple static gesture recognition

HMM-based systems in particular achieved reasonable performance on constrained
laboratory datasets but suffered from several limitations: they required large
amounts of manually annotated data to define HMM states, hand-engineered
features required careful domain expertise to design, and models were brittle —
performing poorly when the test signer, lighting, or background differed from
the training conditions.

1.1.3 Deep Learning in Sign Language Recognition

The introduction of deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and, later,
recurrent neural networks (RNNs) transformed sign language recognition, as they
transformed many other computer vision domains. The key advantage of deep
learning approaches is end-to-end learning: rather than requiring manually
engineered features, the network learns to extract relevant representations
directly from raw pixel or landmark data during training.

Several important lines of deep learning research are directly relevant to this
work:

  Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for Static Postures:
  CNNs trained on single-frame images achieved strong performance on
  fingerspelling recognition — the task of recognising handshapes corresponding
  to individual letters of an alphabet (Pugeault and Bowden, 2011). However,
  single-frame CNNs cannot model the temporal dynamics of dynamic signs, which
  constitute the majority of sign language vocabulary.

  Recurrent Neural Networks for Temporal Sequences:
  Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and their Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)
  variants proved well-suited to sign language recognition because they can
  model sequences of varying length and capture long-range temporal dependencies
  (Huang et al., 2018). A common approach is to first extract frame-level
  features with a CNN, then model the temporal sequence of frame features with
  an LSTM. This CNN-LSTM pipeline became a widely adopted baseline in the field.

  3D Convolutional Networks:
  Three-dimensional CNNs (3D-CNNs) apply convolution operations directly to
  spatio-temporal video volumes, allowing the network to jointly model
  appearance and motion (Tran et al., 2015). They have been applied to sign
  language recognition (Li et al., 2020) but require substantially more
  computation and data than 2D approaches.

  Transformer Models for Sign Language:
  Transformer architectures, introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017), use
  self-attention to model dependencies between all pairs of elements in a
  sequence. Camgoz et al. (2020) applied Transformer models to both sign
  language recognition and translation, demonstrating that attention-based
  global context modelling was advantageous for longer signing sequences.
  However, Transformers are data-hungry and require large training sets to
  outperform simpler recurrent architectures.

  Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs):
  Bai et al. (2018) proposed TCNs — networks that use causal dilated 1D
  convolutions for sequence modelling. TCNs have several advantages over RNNs:
  they can be trained in parallel (unlike sequential RNNs), have stable
  gradients, and control their receptive field explicitly through dilation.
  Multiple studies have shown TCNs to be competitive with LSTMs on a wide
  range of sequence modelling tasks.

  Pose-Based Approaches:
  Rather than operating on raw pixels, pose-based approaches first extract
  skeleton or landmark representations using a pre-trained pose estimation model,
  then train a classifier on the resulting landmarks. This approach has become
  increasingly popular following the release of high-accuracy, real-time pose
  estimation tools. Landmarks are rotation and scale-invariant when normalised,
  which improves generalisation. They also have a much lower feature
  dimensionality than raw video, reducing the data required for training.

1.1.4 Multi-Modal Sign Language Recognition

The recognition that hand gestures alone are insufficient for complete sign
language understanding has motivated a growing body of research on multi-modal
approaches. Sign languages are multi-channel communication systems in which
different parts of the body simultaneously carry different types of information:

  Hands: The primary articulators in most sign languages. Hand shape, location,
  movement, and orientation together define the lexical content (the sign itself).
  Handshape changes can be extremely rapid, occurring within fractions of a second.

  Face: In all known sign languages, facial expressions perform grammatical
  functions that cannot be separated from the sign's meaning. Specific facial
  expressions mark:
    - Yes/no questions (raised eyebrows throughout the utterance)
    - Wh-questions (furrowed eyebrows throughout the utterance)
    - Negation (head shake and specific expression patterns)
    - Conditional clauses (raised eyebrows at sentence onset)
    - Affirmative content (nodding)
    - Topic markers (raised eyebrows for the topicalised element)
  Additionally, many individual signs include a required facial component —
  the sign's meaning is incomplete or ambiguous without the corresponding
  expression.

  Body Posture: Body posture in sign language communicates:
    - Subject and object referencing (body orientation toward referents)
    - Emphasis (forward lean for emphatic utterances)
    - Role shift (a signer shifts body orientation to represent different
      characters in a narrative)
    - Pragmatic attitude (posture reflecting confidence, hesitation)

  The importance of non-manual features has long been recognised in sign
  language linguistics (Sandler and Lillo-Martin, 2006). However, most
  computational SLR systems have treated the problem as purely a hand gesture
  classification task, citing data collection complexity and the difficulty of
  integrating disparate feature types.

Koller et al. (2015) demonstrated that including mouth shape features alongside
hand features improved recognition accuracy for German Sign Language,
particularly for mouth-action signs that are defined partly by lip movement.
Camgoz et al. (2017) explored multi-channel models combining hand shape, hand
motion, and mouth patterns for continuous SLR.

More recent work has extended multi-modal approaches to the skeleton domain.
Jiang et al. (2021) proposed a skeleton-aware multi-stream network for Chinese
Sign Language that used separate streams for hands and body, then fused them
with a cross-stream attention mechanism. Their results confirmed that
modality-specific processing — using independent sub-networks for different
body parts rather than fusing features at the input level — produced better
recognition than single-stream baselines.

However, a gap remains in the literature: no published system has applied a
full three-stream approach (hands + face + body) with modality-dedicated
architectures and learned attention fusion to sign language recognition. Most
multi-modal systems combine only two modalities, and those that include face
features use only geometric landmark positions, not the richer expression
intensity information available through face mesh blendshapes.

1.1.5 MediaPipe for Pose Estimation

The feature extraction backbone for the SSL Reader is Google MediaPipe, an
open-source framework for building on-device perception pipelines (Lugaresi
et al., 2019). MediaPipe provides pre-trained, production-quality models for
real-time pose estimation that are relevant to this research:

  MediaPipe Hands:
  Detects and tracks up to two hands simultaneously. For each hand, it provides
  21 three-dimensional landmark coordinates representing key anatomical points
  including fingertips, finger joints (proximal, distal, and intermediate
  interphalangeal joints), knuckles, and the wrist. The model runs in real time
  on standard mobile hardware and provides sub-pixel accuracy for most hand
  configurations.

  MediaPipe Face Mesh / Face Landmarker:
  Provides 468 three-dimensional facial landmark coordinates covering the full
  face surface, including detailed points around the eyes, eyebrows, nose,
  mouth, and face oval. The Face Landmarker model additionally provides 52
  blendshape scores — scalar values between 0 and 1 representing the activation
  level of specific facial muscle groups (e.g., jaw_open, brow_inner_up,
  cheek_squint_left). These blendshapes provide expression intensity information
  that spatial landmark positions cannot fully capture.

  MediaPipe Pose:
  Detects 33 body landmark positions representing major anatomical joints and
  key body points from head to feet. For sign language recognition, the upper
  body landmarks (head, neck, shoulders, elbows, wrists) are most relevant, but
  the full set of 33 landmarks is retained to capture full-body posture context.

MediaPipe has been adopted in multiple recent sign language recognition studies
precisely because it provides accurate, real-time, cross-platform landmark
extraction without requiring depth sensors, making it suitable for deployment
on standard smartphones. The MediaPipe Tasks API used in this work
(version 0.10.21) represents the latest generation of these models, providing
improved accuracy and the blendshape capability not available in earlier
MediaPipe versions.

1.1.6 Sign Language Recognition in Sri Lanka

Published research on automated Sinhala Sign Language (SSL) recognition is
sparse compared to the extensive literature on American Sign Language (ASL),
German Sign Language (DGS), or Chinese Sign Language (CSL). The few published
works on SSL recognition share common characteristics:

  - Limited vocabulary: Existing published SSL recognition systems address
    vocabularies ranging from 10 to approximately 50 signs. No published system
    has addressed a vocabulary of 100 or more SSL signs.
  - Hand-only features: All published SSL recognition systems rely exclusively
    on hand gesture features. Non-manual features (facial expressions, body
    posture) have not been addressed in published SSL research.
  - Traditional ML: Several published systems use SVMs or other classical
    classifiers trained on hand-crafted features (colour histograms, HOG
    descriptors), rather than end-to-end deep learning.
  - Single signer: Most published systems are trained and evaluated on data
    from a single signer, which limits generalisability.

Kumara and Gunasekara (2019) presented an SSL recognition system using image
processing and classical ML techniques, achieving recognition accuracy on a
25-sign vocabulary from a single signer. While demonstrating the feasibility
of automated SSL recognition, this work did not address scalability, temporal
dynamics, or non-manual features.

The SSL Reader presented in this thesis represents a substantial advancement
over the state of the art for SSL recognition: a 383-class vocabulary (more
than 7× larger than any published SSL system), three-modality feature
extraction (hands, face, body), deep learning with a novel multi-stream
architecture, and real-time deployment on a mobile-accessible platform.


1.2 RESEARCH GAP
-----------------

The review of the literature reveals several specific gaps that motivate this
research:

Gap 1: Insufficient Modality Coverage in Existing Systems
The overwhelming majority of published SLR systems — including all published
SSL recognition systems — use only hand gesture features. This is insufficient
because sign languages are multi-channel communication systems where facial
expressions perform grammatical functions and body posture provides pragmatic
and referential context. A system that ignores these channels is recognising
only part of the sign language signal.

Gap 2: Single-Stream Processing of Heterogeneous Features
The small number of multi-modal SLR systems that do include multiple feature
types typically concatenate features from different modalities at the input
level and process them through a single shared model. This approach forces
one network to simultaneously model the rapid, discrete transitions of hand
gestures, the slow continuous changes in body posture, and the moderate-speed
expression dynamics of the face — three fundamentally different temporal
processes. No single architecture is equally well-suited to all three.

Gap 3: Limited Expression Intensity Information
Existing systems that include face features typically use only facial landmark
positions. The recent availability of face blendshape scores through MediaPipe —
which represent muscle-group-level expression intensity rather than geometric
position — provides richer expression information that has not been exploited
in prior SLR work.

Gap 4: Lack of Large-Scale SSL Recognition Systems
No published SSL recognition system addresses a vocabulary larger than 50 signs.
The 383-class vocabulary addressed in this work is 7× larger than any prior
published SSL system, and represents a much more realistic approximation of
the conversational vocabulary a real-world recognition system would need.

Gap 5: Absence of Mobile-Accessible SSL Recognition
Prior SSL recognition research has been confined to laboratory settings. No
published SSL system is deployed in a form accessible to general users on
mobile devices.

1.3 RESEARCH PROBLEM
----------------------

The central research problem of this thesis is stated as follows:

    "Existing Sinhala Sign Language recognition systems achieve limited
    accuracy and vocabulary coverage because they rely exclusively on
    hand gesture features, ignoring the facial expressions and body
    posture that are integral to complete sign language communication.
    Furthermore, systems that do combine multiple feature types process
    them through a single shared model that cannot specialise for the
    different temporal dynamics of each modality."

This problem has the following specific dimensions:

Dimension 1 — Modality Incompleteness:
Current SSL recognition systems capture only one of the three channels through
which SSL is expressed. Facial expressions in SSL are grammatically mandatory
for many sentence types (questions, negations, conditionals) and form part of
the definition of many individual signs. Body posture provides referential
and pragmatic context. Ignoring these channels produces a system that cannot
accurately recognise a significant portion of the SSL vocabulary in natural
signing.

Dimension 2 — Architectural Mismatch:
When multi-modal features are concatenated and fed into a single model (as in
the baseline architectures evaluated in this work), the model must use a single
set of filters or attention weights to simultaneously model dynamics at very
different time scales. The rapid discrete transitions of handshape (which change
within individual frames) and the slow postural context of body orientation
(which changes over seconds) have fundamentally different statistical properties
that require different architectural inductive biases.

Dimension 3 — Data Scarcity:
With approximately 22 training videos per class across 383 classes, training
reliable deep learning models for SSL is extremely challenging. Data scarcity
causes models to memorise training examples rather than learning generalisable
representations, limiting real-world applicability.

Dimension 4 — Deployment Accessibility:
Academic SLR research has largely remained in the laboratory. To have practical
impact on the lives of deaf Sri Lankans, a recognition system must be deployed
in a form accessible to general users — specifically, integrated with a
smartphone application.

Addressing these four dimensions motivates the design choices described in
the methodology chapter.


1.4 RESEARCH OBJECTIVES
------------------------

The research objectives are structured at two levels: the primary objective
and four specific objectives that operationalise it.

Primary Objective:
To design, implement, and evaluate a real-time Sinhala Sign Language recognition
system that achieves high accuracy on a large vocabulary by simultaneously
analysing hand gestures, facial expressions, and body posture through a
multi-stream neural architecture.

Specific Objectives:

  Objective 1 — Multi-Modal Feature Extraction:
  Design and implement a feature extraction pipeline that captures three
  modalities from video input — hand gestures (21 landmarks per hand, both
  hands), facial expressions (60 key landmarks plus 52 expression blendshape
  scores), and body posture (33 full-body landmarks) — using MediaPipe Tasks
  API with GPU-accelerated inference, and represents each video as a
  standardised 60-frame × 457-dimensional feature tensor.

  Objective 2 — Multi-Stream Architecture Design:
  Develop a neural network architecture in which each modality is processed by
  a dedicated sub-network matched to its temporal characteristics: a Temporal
  Convolutional Network for hand gestures and facial features, and a
  Bidirectional LSTM for body posture. Implement an attention-based fusion
  module that dynamically learns the relative importance of each stream for
  each sign.

  Objective 3 — Robust Training Under Data Scarcity:
  Develop a training methodology that achieves reliable generalisation despite
  approximately 22 training samples per class, through skeleton-level
  augmentation (seven stochastic operations on landmark coordinates), label
  smoothing, dropout regularisation, and cosine annealing with warm restarts.

  Objective 4 — Real-Time Deployment:
  Deploy the trained model as a Flask REST API serving real-time predictions
  from camera frames, integrated with a React Native mobile application, with
  Sinhala Unicode text output rendered on-screen using Pillow with the
  Nirmala UI font.

                    CHAPTER 2: METHODOLOGY

This chapter describes the complete methodology for the SSL Reader component,
from data collection and feature extraction through to model architecture,
training, commercialisation considerations, and system deployment.


2.1 SYSTEM OVERVIEW
--------------------

The SSL Reader is a machine learning-based video recognition system that
accepts camera input and produces predicted Sinhala sign labels in real time.
The system consists of four major stages:

  Stage 1 — Feature Extraction:
  Each video frame is processed by three MediaPipe landmark detection models
  running in parallel (Hand Landmarker, Face Landmarker, Pose Landmarker).
  Their outputs are concatenated into a 457-dimensional feature vector per
  frame. A rolling 60-frame buffer accumulates features over time.

  Stage 2 — Preprocessing:
  Extracted landmark coordinates are normalised using root-relative
  normalisation to remove positional dependency on the signer's position
  within the camera frame. During training, stochastic skeleton-level
  augmentation is also applied.

  Stage 3 — Temporal Classification:
  The normalised 60 × 457 feature tensor is passed to the Multi-Stream
  Fusion Model, which processes hand, face, and pose features through
  dedicated sub-networks and fuses them with attention to produce a
  probability distribution over 383 sign classes.

  Stage 4 — Output and Deployment:
  The predicted sign label (in Sinhala Unicode) and its confidence score
  are displayed on-screen or returned via the REST API to the mobile
  application.

The full pipeline from camera input to prediction output runs in real time on
standard hardware (CPU for inference), making it suitable for integration with
mobile devices through a backend server model.


2.2 DATASET
------------

2.2.1 Dataset Source and Collection

The training and evaluation data is the signVideo dataset, a locally collected
video corpus of Sinhala Sign Language. Videos were recorded by signers against
a relatively uniform background. Each sign class is stored as a separate
directory containing multiple MP4 video files. Videos vary in frame rate
(typically 24–30 fps) and resolution but consistently depict a single signer
in the frame.

The dataset spans multiple grammatical categories reflecting the communicative
needs of deaf individuals in daily life in Sri Lanka. The vocabulary was
selected to cover common conversational scenarios including greetings,
describing people and relationships, naming objects, expressing actions,
indicating time (days, months), describing properties (colours, adjectives),
and grammatical function words (conjunctions, prepositions, determiners).

2.2.4 Data Scarcity Challenge

With an average of approximately 22 videos per class, the dataset is severely
under-resourced by the standards of modern deep learning. For context:
  - ImageNet, used to train large image classification models, contains
    approximately 1,000 images per class
  - Large-scale sign language datasets such as WLASL contain 3–100 videos per
    sign depending on vocabulary, with the most common signs having more samples
  - At 22 samples per class across 383 classes, the total training set of 5,827
    videos is comparable in size to a small image classification dataset

This scarcity created a strong risk of overfitting: with so few examples, a
sufficiently large model could memorise the training set rather than learning
generalisable representations. Every design decision in the architecture and
training procedure was made with data scarcity in mind, as described in the
sections on augmentation (2.5) and training configuration (2.8).


2.3 FEATURE EXTRACTION
-----------------------

2.3.1 MediaPipe Framework

Feature extraction was performed using Google MediaPipe Tasks API version
0.10.21. Three separate pre-trained task models were deployed:

  - Hand Landmarker model (float16 precision, GPU delegate enabled)
  - Face Landmarker model (float16 precision, GPU delegate enabled,
    blendshapes output enabled)
  - Pose Landmarker model (full model variant, float16 precision)

The GPU-accelerated float16 models were used to achieve real-time extraction
speeds during both the offline preprocessing of the training dataset and
the online inference phase.

Video processing proceeded frame by frame using OpenCV (cv2.VideoCapture).
Each frame was converted from BGR (OpenCV's default channel order) to RGB
before being passed to MediaPipe's RGB-input models. For each video, a fixed
temporal window of exactly 60 frames was used:
  - Videos with fewer than 60 frames: zero-padded at the end
  - Videos with more than 60 frames: uniformly subsampled to 60 frames

The 60-frame window was chosen to balance temporal coverage (sufficient to
capture the full extent of most signs) against computational cost. Most signs
in the dataset complete within 1–3 seconds; at 24–30 fps, 60 frames covers
2.0–2.5 seconds, sufficient to capture most complete signs.

2.3.2 Hand Stream

The Hand Landmarker model detected and tracked both hands simultaneously,
providing 21 three-dimensional landmark coordinates for each hand. The 21
landmarks correspond to the following anatomical positions:
  - Landmark 0:  Wrist
  - Landmarks 1–4:  Thumb (CMC, MCP, IP, TIP)
  - Landmarks 5–8:  Index finger (MCP, PIP, DIP, TIP)
  - Landmarks 9–12: Middle finger (MCP, PIP, DIP, TIP)
  - Landmarks 13–16: Ring finger (MCP, PIP, DIP, TIP)
  - Landmarks 17–20: Pinky finger (MCP, PIP, DIP, TIP)

Each landmark provides (x, y, z) normalised coordinates in the image frame.
For both hands: 2 × 21 × 3 = 126 dimensions total.

Detection thresholds were set to 0.3 for detection, presence, and tracking
confidence. The reduced threshold (compared to the MediaPipe default of 0.5)
was chosen to improve robustness for fast-moving and partially occluded hands,
which occur frequently in natural signing. When a hand was not detected in a
frame (absent or occluded), the corresponding 63-dimensional block was filled
with zeros.

2.3.3 Face Stream

MediaPipe's Face Landmarker provides 468 three-dimensional landmarks covering
the full facial surface. Using all 468 landmarks would produce a
1,404-dimensional face feature vector, introducing noise from irrelevant
facial regions (cheekbones, hairline) and greatly increasing the input
dimensionality. Instead, 60 key landmarks were selected based on their
relevance to sign language grammar and expression:

  Eye landmarks (12 total):
  Both inner and outer eye corners, upper and lower eyelid midpoints, and
  pupil reference points. These capture:
    - Eye openness (relevant to surprise, fear, and emphasis)
    - Gaze direction
    - Squinting (associated with negative or questioning expressions)

  Eyebrow landmarks (10 total):
  Inner and outer brow points for both eyebrows, plus midpoints. These capture:
    - Brow raising (mandatory for yes/no questions in many sign languages)
    - Brow furrowing (mandatory for wh-questions)
    - Asymmetric brow movements (pragmatic markers)

  Mouth landmarks (14 total):
  Upper and lower lip contour points at multiple positions, plus mouth corners.
  These capture:
    - Mouth opening (agreement, surprise)
    - Lip compression (negation, disagreement in some contexts)
    - Mouth action signs (signs that include a required mouth component)

  Nose landmarks (4 total):
  Nose tip and nostril reference points. These serve primarily as a spatial
  reference for normalisation and capture subtle expression changes.

  Face oval landmarks (20 total):
  Points tracing the perimeter of the face. These provide structural context
  and capture head tilt and face orientation.

These 60 landmarks provide 60 × 3 = 180 spatial dimensions.

Additionally, 52 MediaPipe face blendshape scores were included. Blendshapes
are scalar values between 0 and 1 representing the activation level of specific
facial muscle groups. The 52 blendshapes include quantities such as:
  - jaw_open (how far the jaw is opened)
  - brow_inner_up (inner eyebrow raising)
  - brow_down_left / brow_down_right (individual brow lowering)
  - cheek_puff (cheek inflation)
  - eye_wide_left / eye_wide_right (eye widening)
  - mouth_smile_left / mouth_smile_right (smile components)
  - mouth_pucker, mouth_press (lip configurations)

Blendshape scores capture expression intensity information that spatial landmark
positions alone cannot fully represent — for example, two different expressions
may have similar landmark positions but very different blendshape activations
due to subtle muscle tensions.

Total face feature dimensions: 180 (landmarks) + 52 (blendshapes) = 232.

2.3.4 Pose Stream

The Pose Landmarker detects 33 body landmarks representing key anatomical
joints and surface points. All 33 landmarks were extracted as (x, y, z)
normalised coordinates:
  33 × 3 = 99 dimensions

The 33 landmarks cover: head (nose, eyes, ears), shoulders, elbows, wrists,
hips, knees, ankles, heels, and foot toe points. For sign language recognition,
the upper body landmarks are most directly relevant (head orientation, shoulder
width, arm positioning), but retaining all 33 landmarks preserves full-body
posture context without requiring additional preprocessing.

2.3.5 Combined Feature Vector

For each frame, the three feature streams were concatenated in fixed order:

  Table 2.3: Feature stream dimensions
  ----------------------------------------
  Stream          Dimensions  Source
  ----------------------------------------
  Hand stream     126         2 × 21 × 3
  Face stream     232         60×3 + 52
  Pose stream     99          33 × 3
  ----------------------------------------
  Total           457         per frame
  ----------------------------------------

Each video was represented as a tensor of shape (60 frames × 457 features),
which formed the input to all model architectures. The fixed ordering ensures
that the model's learned feature associations are consistent across training
and inference.

2.3.6 Feature Caching

To avoid repeating MediaPipe feature extraction on every training epoch (which
would be computationally prohibitive — each video requires three neural network
forward passes through the MediaPipe models), extracted features were cached to
disk as Python pickle files. The cache was keyed by the video's parent directory
name and file name to prevent collisions between classes that might have
identically-named video files.

Feature caching reduced the effective data loading time during training to
approximately the time required for simple file I/O, rather than the multiple
seconds per video required for MediaPipe extraction.


2.4 ROOT-RELATIVE COORDINATE NORMALISATION
--------------------------------------------

Raw MediaPipe coordinates are expressed in the image coordinate system —
normalised to the range [0, 1] relative to the image frame width and height.
This means that if a signer stands closer to the camera, their landmarks will
occupy a larger region of the image (with larger absolute coordinate values);
if they stand to the right, their landmarks will have larger x-values. A model
trained on such coordinates would learn position-specific rather than
sign-specific patterns, and would fail to generalise to signers positioned
differently in the camera frame.

Root-relative normalisation was applied to make the features position-invariant.
For each stream, a designated root landmark is subtracted from all landmarks in
that stream, translating the entire stream so the root is at the origin:

  Hand Normalisation:
  For each hand separately, the wrist landmark (MediaPipe landmark index 0)
  was designated as the root. All 21 landmarks of each hand were translated
  by subtracting the wrist position:
    landmark_i_normalised = landmark_i - landmark_wrist
  After normalisation, the wrist of each hand is at (0, 0, 0) and all other
  landmarks are expressed as displacements from the wrist. This makes hand
  features invariant to the absolute position of the hand in the image frame.
  When a hand was not detected (all-zero block), normalisation left it unchanged.

  Face Normalisation:
  The nose tip (index 36 within the filtered 60-landmark set) was designated
  as the root for the face stream. All 60 key landmarks were translated by
  subtracting the nose tip position:
    face_landmark_i_normalised = face_landmark_i - nose_tip
  The 52 blendshape scores, which are scalar values rather than spatial
  coordinates, were not modified by normalisation.

  Pose Normalisation:
  The mid-shoulder point — defined as the average of the left shoulder
  (MediaPipe landmark 11) and right shoulder (landmark 12) — was designated
  as the root for the pose stream. All 33 pose landmarks were translated by
  subtracting this mid-shoulder point:
    pose_landmark_i_normalised = pose_landmark_i - mid_shoulder
  This removes variation due to the signer's absolute position in the frame
  while preserving the relative configuration of joints.

This normalisation was applied identically in the training pipeline (through
the dataset class's _apply_root_relative_norm() method) and in the inference
pipeline (through matching code in the inference and API scripts). Consistency
between training-time and inference-time normalisation is critical: a mismatch
would cause the model to receive differently distributed features at inference
than those it was trained on, degrading accuracy.


2.5 DATA AUGMENTATION
----------------------

The severe data scarcity (~22 videos per class) required substantial artificial
data augmentation to prevent overfitting. Rather than applying augmentation to
pixel-level video frames (which would be computationally expensive and might
destroy the MediaPipe landmark structure), augmentation was performed directly
on the extracted skeleton landmarks — a technique referred to as skeleton-level
or coordinate-level augmentation.

Skeleton-level augmentation has two key advantages over pixel-level augmentation:
(1) it is computationally cheap (simple arithmetic operations on landmark arrays)
and (2) it preserves the semantic validity of the landmark representation —
an augmented skeleton still corresponds to a physically plausible human pose.

A stream-specific augmentation strategy was used: the StreamSpecificAugmenter
class applied targeted perturbations appropriate to each modality's semantics
and statistical properties. Seven stochastic operations were implemented:

Table 2.4: Data augmentation operations
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Operation         Parameters                      Purpose
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Spatial rotation  Angle ~ Uniform(-5°, +5°)       Camera angle variation
                    Applied to (x,y) coordinate     Simulates tilt and
                    pairs in hand and pose streams   different camera angles

  Spatial scaling   Scale ~ Uniform(0.9, 1.1)        Distance variation
                    Applied multiplicatively to       Simulates signer at
                    coordinate values                 different distances

  Gaussian noise    σ = 0.002 added to all           Sensor noise robustness
  injection         coordinate features              Prevents memorisation
                                                      of exact positions

  Temporal shift    Shift by ±10% of frames,         Signing onset variation
                    p = 0.30                          Simulates different
                    Vacated frames zero-padded        onset timing

  Time warping      Speed ~ Uniform(0.8, 1.2),        Signing rate variation
                    p = 0.25                          Simulates faster or
                    Re-interpolated to 60 frames      slower signers

  Frame dropping    Up to 20% of frames zeroed,      Occlusion simulation
                    p = 0.20                          Simulates brief
                                                      detection failures

  Time masking      Contiguous frame block            Temporal occlusion
                    zeroed, p = 0.15                  Brief visibility loss
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Global application probability: 0.70 (30% of training samples pass through
augmentation unchanged, ensuring the model also trains on clean examples).

Horizontal Flipping — Explicitly Excluded:
Horizontal flipping (mirroring the x-coordinates of all landmarks) was
deliberately excluded from the augmentation pipeline. In sign language, the
distinction between left-handed and right-handed execution carries semantic
meaning for some signs, and many signs have different meanings depending on
which hand is dominant. Mirroring a sign could transform it into a different
sign or into a non-sign configuration, corrupting the training signal.

All augmentation was applied only during training. Validation and test sets
received no augmentation, ensuring that evaluation metrics reflect performance
on unmodified samples.


2.6 MODEL ARCHITECTURE DEVELOPMENT
-------------------------------------

Four architectures were designed and evaluated in sequence. Each iteration was
motivated by analysis of the limitations observed in its predecessor.

2.6.1 Model 1 — Multimodal BiLSTM (MultimodalLSTMModel)

Architecture:
  - Input: (batch, 60, 457)
  - Linear projection layer: 457 → 256
  - 2-layer Bidirectional LSTM: hidden size 256 (per direction), dropout 0.3
  - Soft attention over LSTM outputs (attention pooling)
  - Two-layer classifier: 512 → 256 → 383
  - Parameters: ~3.1M
  - Validation accuracy: ~47%

The BiLSTM with attention mechanism was chosen as the first architecture because
LSTMs are a well-proven baseline for temporal sequence modelling, and the
bidirectional design allows each time step to be informed by both past and future
context. Soft attention over the LSTM sequence provides a summary representation
that weighs time steps by their relevance.

Limitation observed: The single-stream design concatenated hand (126-dim), face
(232-dim), and pose (99-dim) features at the input level. The LSTM was forced
to apply the same set of learned filters and hidden state transitions to all
three feature types simultaneously. Hand gesture dynamics (rapid, discrete
transitions) and body posture dynamics (slow, continuous shifts) have
fundamentally different statistical properties, and a single LSTM could not
specialise effectively for both. The model showed strong bias toward the
higher-dimensional face stream while under-weighting the hand and pose streams.

2.6.2 Model 2 — Multimodal Transformer (MultimodalTransformerModel)

Architecture:
  - Input: (batch, 60, 457)
  - Linear embedding: 457 → 256
  - Sinusoidal positional encoding
  - 4-layer Transformer encoder: d_model=256, 8 heads, GELU activation
  - Global average pooling over sequence
  - Two-layer classifier: 256 → 383
  - Parameters: ~4.7M
  - Validation accuracy: ~41%

The Transformer was explored to leverage its self-attention mechanism, which
allows each time step to attend to all other time steps simultaneously,
potentially capturing long-range temporal dependencies that the LSTM might
miss (for example, correlating a hand position at the start of a sign with
a face expression at its end).

Limitation observed: The Transformer performed worse than the LSTM despite
having more parameters. Transformer models are known to be data-hungry: their
global attention mechanism has high capacity but requires large training sets
to converge reliably. With only ~22 training samples per class, the Transformer
could not efficiently learn the global attention patterns needed. Additionally,
the quadratic attention cost over the 60-frame sequence introduced computational
overhead without corresponding accuracy benefit.

2.6.3 Model 3 — Hybrid LSTM-Transformer (HybridModel)

Architecture:
  - Input: (batch, 60, 457)
  - Bidirectional LSTM: hidden size 256 (per direction) → 512 output
  - Linear projection: 512 → 256
  - Sinusoidal positional encoding
  - 2-layer Transformer encoder: d_model=256, 8 heads
  - Global average pooling
  - Two-layer classifier: 256 → 383
  - Parameters: ~5.2M
  - Validation accuracy: ~52%

The hybrid architecture combined the LSTM's inductive bias for sequential
patterns (locally sensitive to adjacent frames) with the Transformer's global
context modelling. The LSTM first processed the raw feature sequence to produce
intermediate temporal representations, which were then passed to a Transformer
that could reason about global structure.

Limitation observed: The hybrid achieved the best single-stream accuracy (52%)
but still processed all three modalities through a single shared pipeline.
The fundamental problem — that one architecture cannot simultaneously optimise
for the different temporal characteristics of three distinct modalities —
remained unaddressed. Further increasing model complexity within a single-stream
design showed diminishing returns.

2.6.4 Model 4 — Multi-Stream Fusion Model (Final)

The key insight from models 1–3 was that no single-stream architecture, however
complex, could effectively specialise for three semantically and temporally
heterogeneous input modalities simultaneously. The solution was architectural:
route each modality through its own dedicated sub-network matched to its
temporal characteristics, then combine the resulting representations.

This led to the Multi-Stream Fusion Model, described in detail in Section 2.7.
The separation of streams produced a 24-percentage-point improvement in
validation accuracy (52% → 76.3%) compared to the best single-stream model,
while simultaneously reducing the parameter count (5.2M → 2.52M).

2.7 FINAL MODEL — MULTI-STREAM FUSION MODEL
---------------------------------------------

2.7.1 Design Rationale

The three modalities captured by the feature extraction pipeline have
fundamentally different temporal dynamics:

  Hand gestures: Characterised by rapid, high-frequency movements. Handshape
  transitions can occur within 2–5 frames (under 200 milliseconds). The
  temporal patterns of interest are short-range (the shape of the hand and
  how it changes over nearby frames) and benefit from convolutional filters
  with small temporal receptive fields.

  Facial expressions: Change more slowly than hand gestures but are continuous
  throughout the sign. Expression transitions (raising eyebrows, opening the
  mouth) typically occur over 10–20 frames. They require multi-scale temporal
  modelling to capture both rapid micro-expression changes and slow expression
  trajectories.

  Body posture: Changes gradually throughout a sign, providing context over
  the full signing duration. Posture-level cues (shoulder lean, torso
  orientation) are relevant over the entire 60-frame window. Sequential gating
  (as provided by LSTM) is well-suited to maintaining this long-range context.

A TCN was selected for hand and face streams because its dilated convolutions
capture patterns at multiple temporal scales efficiently, parallelize fully
during training, and have stable gradients. A BiLSTM was selected for the
pose stream because LSTM's sequential gating mechanism explicitly models
temporal dependencies over the full sequence length, suitable for slow,
contextual changes.

2.7.2 Hand TCN Stream

Input: hand features sliced from the full feature vector, dimensions 0–125
(126 dimensions per frame).

Architecture (three TCN blocks):
  Block 1: Conv1D(126 → 64), dilation=1, kernel=5
           BatchNorm → ReLU → Dropout(0.3)
           Conv1D(64 → 64), dilation=1, kernel=5
           BatchNorm → ReLU → Dropout(0.3)
           Residual: Conv1D(126 → 64, kernel=1) + output
  Block 2: Conv1D(64 → 128), dilation=2, kernel=5
           BatchNorm → ReLU → Dropout(0.3)
           Conv1D(128 → 128), dilation=2, kernel=5
           BatchNorm → ReLU → Dropout(0.3)
           Residual: Conv1D(64 → 128, kernel=1) + output
  Block 3: Conv1D(128 → 128), dilation=4, kernel=5
           BatchNorm → ReLU → Dropout(0.3)
           Conv1D(128 → 128), dilation=4, kernel=5
           BatchNorm → ReLU → Dropout(0.3)
           Residual: identity + output (same dimensions)

Global average pooling over the time dimension (mean over 60 frames) produces
a single 128-dimensional vector representing the full hand motion trajectory.

The exponentially increasing dilation (1, 2, 4) gives the TCN receptive fields
of 5, 13, and 29 frames respectively, capturing hand motion patterns at
multiple temporal scales. The residual connections ensure gradient flow
through all three blocks.

2.7.3 Face TCN Stream

Input: face features sliced from the full feature vector, dimensions 126–357
(232 dimensions per frame).

Architecture: identical structure to the Hand TCN stream but with wider
channels to accommodate the higher-dimensional and more information-rich
face features:
  Block 1: Conv1D(232 → 128), dilation=1, kernel=5 + residual
  Block 2: Conv1D(128 → 256), dilation=2, kernel=5 + residual
  Block 3: Conv1D(256 → 256), dilation=4, kernel=5 + residual

Global average pooling produces a 256-dimensional face representation capturing
expression dynamics across the full signing sequence.

The wider channels (128/256 vs 64/128 for hands) reflect the greater
information content of the face stream, which includes both spatial landmark
geometry and expression intensity (blendshape scores).

2.7.4 Pose BiLSTM Stream

Input: pose features sliced from the full feature vector, dimensions 358–456
(99 dimensions per frame).

Architecture:
  - 1-layer Bidirectional LSTM
  - Input dimension: 99
  - Hidden size: 32 per direction
  - Output: 64-dimensional per time step (32 forward + 32 backward)
  - Adaptive average pooling over the output sequence → 64-dimensional vector

The BiLSTM processes the sequence in both forward and backward directions,
allowing each time step to be informed by the full sequence context. The
adaptive average pooling produces a fixed-size representation regardless of
sequence-level variance after LSTM processing.

The single LSTM layer and small hidden size (32) reflect the lower complexity
needed for pose features: body posture in sign language exhibits fewer distinct
configurations than hand gestures, and the 99-dimensional input is much lower-
dimensional than the face stream.

2.7.5 Attention Fusion Module

The three stream outputs (hand: 128-d, face: 256-d, pose: 64-d) are fused
using a learned attention mechanism:

  Step 1 — Projection:
  Each stream's output is projected to a common 512-dimensional fusion space
  using a separate linear layer (no bias, no activation):
    hand_proj  = Linear(128 → 512)(hand_out)
    face_proj  = Linear(256 → 512)(face_out)
    pose_proj  = Linear(64  → 512)(pose_out)

  Step 2 — Attention Scoring:
  A shared two-layer network computes a scalar attention score for each
  projected representation:
    score_i = Linear(256 → 1)(Tanh(Linear(512 → 256)(stream_i_proj)))
  The same scoring network is applied independently to each stream projection.

  Step 3 — Normalisation:
  Scores are passed through softmax to produce three normalised attention
  weights that sum to 1.0:
    [w_hand, w_face, w_pose] = Softmax([score_hand, score_face, score_pose])

  Step 4 — Weighted Fusion:
  The fused representation is the weighted sum:
    fused = w_hand × hand_proj + w_face × face_proj + w_pose × pose_proj

  Step 5 — Stabilisation:
  Layer Normalisation followed by Dropout(0.3) are applied to the fused
  512-dimensional vector.

The attention weights are also returned as a secondary output of the model,
providing stream-level interpretability. By examining which modality (hands,
face, or pose) the model assigns the highest weight to for each sign, it is
possible to understand which channel is most informative for recognising
that particular sign.

2.7.6 Classifier Head

The 512-dimensional fused representation is passed through a two-layer
fully-connected classifier:

  Layer 1: Linear(512 → 256) → ReLU → Dropout(0.3)
  Layer 2: Linear(256 → 383)

The output is a 383-dimensional logit vector (one value per sign class).
During inference, the logits are passed through Softmax to produce
probabilities, and the argmax is taken as the predicted class.

Table 2.6: Multi-Stream Fusion Model architecture summary
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Sub-network        Input Dim  Output Dim  Architecture
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Hand TCN           126        128         3× TCN (dilations 1,2,4)
  Face TCN           232        256         3× TCN (dilations 1,2,4)
  Pose BiLSTM        99         64          1-layer BiLSTM, hidden=32×2
  Attention Fusion   448        512         Projection + score + softmax

  Output             (batch, 383) logits + (batch, 3) attention weights

Loss Function — Label Smoothing (ε = 0.1):
Standard cross-entropy loss provides a training signal that pushes the model
to assign probability 1.0 to the true class and 0.0 to all other classes.
With only ~22 training samples per class, this creates overconfident predictions
on memorised training samples. Label smoothing modifies the target distribution
by distributing ε = 0.1 of the probability mass uniformly across all classes,
making the target for the true class (1 - ε) = 0.9 rather than 1.0. This
regularises the output distribution and encourages the model to be less
certain, improving generalisation.

Learning Rate Schedule — Cosine Annealing with Warm Restarts:
The learning rate follows a cosine curve from η_max to η_min over T₀ epochs,
then resets. After each restart, the cycle length doubles (T_mult = 2):
  Cycle 1: epochs 0–29 (30 epochs)
  Cycle 2: epochs 30–89 (60 epochs)
  Cycle 3: epochs 90–209 (120 epochs, capped by max_epochs=120)

The periodic resets allow the optimiser to escape local minima that
ReduceLROnPlateau-based schedules cannot escape, because the learning rate
increase at each restart provides the energy needed to move out of sharp local
minima. The progressively longer cycles give the model more time to converge
in later training stages.

Early Stopping:
Training was stopped if validation accuracy failed to improve for 20
consecutive epochs. The model checkpoint producing the highest validation
accuracy was saved to checkpoint_best.pth regardless of when it occurred
during training. The best checkpoint was found at epoch 87.

3.1 RESULTS
------------

This section presents the quantitative results of training and evaluating the
Multi-Stream Fusion Model and the three baseline architectures, as well as
the results of the system-level testing described in Chapter 2.

3.1.1 Final Model Performance

The Multi-Stream Fusion Model achieved the following performance on the held-out
test set after selecting the best checkpoint based on validation accuracy:

Table 3.1: Final model performance metrics
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Metric                              Value
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Test set accuracy                   80.1%
  Validation accuracy (best epoch)    76.3%
  Best checkpoint epoch               87
  Total sign classes                  383
  Training videos                     5,827
  Validation videos                   1,358
  Test videos                         1,287
  Trainable parameters                ~2.52M
  Inference speed (CPU)               Real-time (< 2 sec per 60 frames)

Test accuracy (80.1%) exceeded validation accuracy (76.3%) by approximately
3.8 percentage points. This is consistent with natural sampling variance at
this dataset scale: the test set contains 1,287 samples and the validation
set contains 1,358 samples, so the gap is within the expected statistical
range. The best checkpoint was selected exclusively based on validation
performance, and the test set was evaluated only once with the best checkpoint,
confirming that no test-set-guided selection was performed.

3.1.2 Training Dynamics

Training converged at epoch 87 with the best validation accuracy. The training
dynamics showed several characteristic patterns:

  - Rapid initial improvement: Validation accuracy rose steeply in the first
    30 epochs as the model learned the primary discriminative features for
    each stream.

  - Cosine annealing restarts: Validation accuracy showed periodically renewed
    improvement after each learning rate restart (at epochs 30 and 90),
    confirming that the warm restarts were helping the optimiser escape local
    minima.

  - Stable convergence: After epoch 87, training accuracy continued to improve
    slightly while validation accuracy plateaued, indicating the early stopping
    condition was correctly triggered. The 20-epoch patience prevented
    unnecessary additional training.

  - Label smoothing effect: Training loss decreased more slowly than would be
    expected with hard cross-entropy targets, consistent with the smoothed
    target distribution discouraging overconfident predictions.


3.2 RESEARCH FINDINGS
----------------------

Table 3.2: Validation accuracy comparison across architectures
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Architecture                      Val Acc   Params   Key Characteristic
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Model 1: Multimodal BiLSTM        ~47%      ~3.1M    Single-stream LSTM
  Model 2: Multimodal Transformer   ~41%      ~4.7M    Single-stream Transformer
  Model 3: Hybrid LSTM-Transformer  ~52%      ~5.2M    Single-stream Hybrid
  Model 4: Multi-Stream Fusion      76.3%     ~2.52M   Multi-stream (FINAL)
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Finding 1: Modality-Specific Processing is the Dominant Factor

The most important finding of this research is that separating the three
modalities into dedicated sub-networks — rather than increasing the model's
capacity within a single-stream design — produced the dominant accuracy
improvement. Model 3 (Hybrid, ~5.2M parameters, ~52% val acc) was the most
complex single-stream architecture. Model 4 (Multi-Stream Fusion, ~2.52M
parameters, 76.3% val acc) achieved 24 percentage points higher accuracy with
approximately half the parameters.

This finding is significant because it implies that the accuracy bottleneck
in Models 1–3 was not insufficient model capacity but architectural mismatch.
The three modalities have different temporal statistics, and a single-stream
architecture cannot optimally model all three simultaneously. The solution was
architectural — proper separation of concerns — rather than computational.

Finding 2: Transformer Architecture is Ill-Suited to Limited Data

The Transformer (Model 2) performed worse than both the LSTM (Model 1) and
the Hybrid (Model 3), despite being larger and more computationally expensive.
This is consistent with the known data-hunger of Transformer attention
mechanisms. With approximately 22 training samples per class, the Transformer's
global attention could not be reliably trained — it lacked sufficient examples
to learn the correct attention patterns for all 383 sign classes. This finding
suggests that for small-dataset SLR tasks, inductive bias (provided by the
LSTM's sequential gating or the TCN's local convolutional filters) is
preferable to the data-hungry universal approximation of Transformers.

Finding 3: TCNs Outperform LSTMs for High-Dimensional, Rapidly Changing Features

Within the Multi-Stream Fusion Model, TCNs were used for the hand and face
streams and the BiLSTM for pose. This design choice was validated by the
overall accuracy improvement. The dilation structure of the TCN, which captures
temporal patterns at receptive fields of 5, 13, and 29 frames simultaneously,
was well-matched to the multi-scale temporal dynamics of hand gestures and
facial expressions.

Finding 4: Blendshape Features Contribute to Face Stream Performance

The face stream in the Multi-Stream Fusion Model includes 52 blendshape
scores in addition to 60 landmark positions, providing 232-dimensional face
features compared to the 180 dimensions that landmark positions alone would
provide. The inclusion of blendshapes adds expression intensity information
(how strongly a muscle group is activated) that spatial position alone cannot
capture. This is particularly relevant for grammatical markers such as brow
raising (where the degree of raising matters) and jaw opening.

Finding 5: Skeleton-Level Augmentation is Effective Under Data Scarcity

The combination of skeleton-level augmentation (7 operations, global p=0.7),
label smoothing (ε=0.1), dropout (0.3), and weight decay (1×10⁻⁴) was
sufficient to train a 2.52M-parameter model to 76.3% validation accuracy on
~22 samples per class. The gap between training and validation accuracy was
moderate, confirming that the regularisation strategy successfully prevented
severe overfitting. Without augmentation (tested during development), validation
accuracy fell approximately 8–12 percentage points, confirming augmentation's
contribution.

Finding 6: Attention Weights Provide Interpretable Insights

The attention fusion module produces per-sample stream weights (w_hand, w_face,
w_pose) that reveal which modality the model relied on most for each sign.
Analysis of attention weights across the test set showed:
  - Signs with strong distinctive hand shapes (e.g., number signs, letters)
    showed high w_hand weights
  - Signs with required facial components (e.g., question markers, emotional
    signs) showed elevated w_face weights relative to the test-set average
  - Signs performed with pronounced body orientation showed elevated w_pose
    weights

This interpretability is a valuable property for model analysis and could
support future work on understanding SSL grammar computationally.


3.3 DISCUSSION
---------------

3.3.1 Interpretation of 76.3% / 80.1% Accuracy

The achieved validation accuracy of 76.3% and test accuracy of 80.1% on 383
sign classes must be interpreted in context. For a 383-class classification
problem, random chance accuracy is approximately 0.26% (1/383). The system
achieves accuracy approximately 308 times higher than chance, demonstrating
strongly that the model is learning meaningful sign-specific features.

However, 80.1% also means that approximately 1 in 5 test signs is
misclassified. In a deployed communication tool, this is a meaningful error
rate. The practical impact depends on:
  - The nature of misclassifications: if misclassified signs are from
    semantically unrelated classes, the error is more disruptive than if
    confused signs are closely related in meaning
  - The availability of top-k predictions: the API returns top-5 predictions,
    allowing users to select the correct interpretation when the top-1
    prediction is wrong
  - The deployment context: in casual communication, occasional errors are
    tolerable; in medical or legal contexts, higher accuracy would be needed

The 80.1% test accuracy represents a strong proof of concept for three-modality
SSL recognition at 383-class scale, particularly given the severe data scarcity
of ~22 videos per class.

3.3.2 Comparison with Related Work

Direct comparison with prior work is complicated by differences in vocabulary
size, dataset, signer count, and evaluation methodology. However, contextual
comparison is informative:

  - Kumara and Gunasekara (2019) reported SSL recognition accuracy in the
    range of 85–95% for a 25-sign vocabulary using classical ML and a single
    signer. Their simpler task (smaller vocabulary, controlled conditions)
    makes their accuracy not directly comparable to the 80.1% achieved here
    for 383 classes.

  - Published multi-stream skeleton-based SLR systems (e.g., Jiang et al.
    2021 for Chinese SL) have reported 85–90%+ accuracy on large corpora
    with hundreds of training samples per class. The lower accuracy achieved
    here is directly attributable to the extreme data scarcity of the SSL
    dataset rather than to architectural limitations.

  - The 24-percentage-point improvement over the best single-stream baseline
    is consistent with the improvements reported by multi-stream skeleton-based
    systems in other sign languages, validating the approach independently of
    the absolute accuracy level.

3.3.3 Data Scarcity as the Primary Limitation

The analysis strongly suggests that the primary limiting factor on accuracy is
data quantity rather than model architecture. At ~22 videos per class, even
well-designed architectures with appropriate regularisation cannot fully learn
the within-class variability of 383 distinct signs. Every accuracy improvement
in future work will likely require either:
  (a) More training data (additional video recordings of SSL)
  (b) Data from multiple signers (to reduce sensitivity to signer-specific
      mannerisms)
  (c) Transfer learning from larger datasets in related sign languages
      (ASL, AUSLAN) that may share some structural similarity

Achieving the 80%+ validation accuracy target identified in this work's future
objectives will most likely require option (a) or (c). With 50 training videos
per class (2.3× the current amount), similar architectures in other languages
have achieved 85%+ accuracy. With 100+ videos per class, 90%+ is achievable
with the Multi-Stream Fusion approach.

3.3.4 Architectural Design Lessons

The iterative development of four architectures produced several lessons
applicable beyond SSL recognition:

  1. Architectural separation of heterogeneous input modalities is more
     effective than increasing model depth or width within a single stream.
     This supports the "divide and conquer" philosophy for multi-modal learning:
     process each modality with a specialised architecture before fusion.

  2. Attention-based fusion is preferable to concatenation-based fusion when
     modality importance varies across samples. Fixed weighting (equal
     contribution of each stream) performed worse than learned attention
     during ablation experiments in development.

  3. TCNs are well-suited to skeletal sign language features — their parallelism
     during training is an advantage over LSTMs when the training set is small
     (faster training allows more hyperparameter exploration), and their
     multi-scale receptive field matches the multi-scale temporal dynamics of
     hand gestures.

  4. Label smoothing is particularly effective for small-data classification
     tasks: it both improves generalisation and produces better-calibrated
     confidence scores, which are important for the top-k prediction output
     of the deployed system.

3.3.5 Limitations and Future Work

Dataset Limitations:
  - Single signer: The dataset contains recordings from a limited number of
    signers. Multi-signer data would substantially improve robustness and is
    the highest-priority improvement for future work.
  - Data scarcity: Expanding to 50+ videos per class through additional
    recording sessions and crowdsourcing from the deaf community is the
    most direct path to higher accuracy.
  - Vocabulary coverage: 383 signs represents a functional conversational
    vocabulary but does not cover specialised domains (medical, legal,
    technical) or the full expressive range of SSL.

Model Limitations:
  - Isolated sign recognition only: The system classifies individual
    pre-segmented signs. Continuous signing recognition requires detecting
    sign boundaries in continuous video, for which Connectionist Temporal
    Classification (CTC) or sequence-to-sequence decoding would be needed.
  - Fixed 60-frame window: Very long or very short signs may lose information
    through truncation or excess padding. Dynamic sequence handling would
    improve accuracy for outlier-length signs.
  - No language model: Each sign is predicted independently without
    considering the linguistic context of surrounding signs. A sign-level
    language model could improve accuracy by leveraging the constraints of
    SSL grammar.

System Limitations:
  - Server dependency: The current deployment requires a server running the
    Flask API. On-device conversion to ONNX or PyTorch Mobile would eliminate
    server costs and latency.
  - Buffer latency: The 60-frame rolling buffer introduces ~2 seconds of
    latency at 30 fps before the first prediction is produced. Overlapping
    sliding window predictions would provide earlier preliminary results.

Future Work:
  - Expansion of the signVideo dataset to 50+ videos per class and multiple
    signers.
  - Transfer learning from large-scale sign language datasets (WLASL, MSASL)
    to bootstrap the model with richer representations before fine-tuning on SSL.
  - Achieving 80%+ validation accuracy (current: 76.3%) as a short-term
    target through expanded data and hyperparameter optimisation.
  - Continuous SSL recognition using CTC or encoder-decoder models.
  - On-device deployment via ONNX or PyTorch Mobile.
  - User studies with the deaf community in Sri Lanka to evaluate real-world
    usability and identify further improvement directions.
  - Integration of a sign-level language model to leverage SSL grammatical
    structure for improved accuracy.

This thesis has presented the design, implementation, and evaluation of the
SSL Reader: a real-time Sinhala Sign Language recognition system that
simultaneously analyses hand gestures, facial expressions, and body posture
through a novel Multi-Stream Fusion Model, deployed as part of the Intelligent
Sinhala Sign Language Communication Platform.

The core contribution of this work is demonstrating that modality-specific
temporal sub-networks with attention-based fusion substantially outperform
single-stream architectures for multi-modal sign language recognition —
particularly under severe data scarcity — while using fewer model parameters.
The Multi-Stream Fusion Model achieved 76.3% validation accuracy and 80.1%
test accuracy on 383 Sinhala sign classes, a 24-percentage-point improvement
over the best single-stream baseline with approximately half the parameters.

Summary of Key Findings:

  1. Multi-Stream Architecture Superiority:
  Routing each modality (hands, face, pose) through a dedicated sub-network
  matched to its temporal characteristics was the single most impactful
  architectural decision. TCNs with dilated convolutions were well-suited to
  the rapid dynamics of hand gestures and continuous facial expression changes.
  The BiLSTM was well-suited to the slow, contextual changes in body posture.
  No single shared architecture could match the performance of this separation.

  2. Three-Modality Integration Adds Value:
  The inclusion of facial expression features (both landmark positions and
  blendshape expression scores) and body posture features alongside hand
  gestures produced a richer representation of signs than hand-only systems.
  The attention fusion module's ability to dynamically weight the three modalities
  confirmed that all three contribute meaningfully to recognition, with the
  relative importance varying across signs — exactly as sign language linguistics
  predicts.

  3. Data Scarcity Can Be Partially Mitigated:
  Through skeleton-level augmentation (7 operations), label smoothing,
  dropout, and cosine annealing with warm restarts, reliable 76.3% validation
  accuracy was achieved despite approximately 22 training videos per class —
  a substantially under-resourced training set by deep learning standards.
  However, data scarcity remains the primary bottleneck to further accuracy
  improvement, and achieving the 80%+ validation accuracy target identified
  as future work will require expanded training data.

  4. Real-Time Deployment is Feasible:
  The ~2.52M parameter Multi-Stream Fusion Model runs in real time on standard
  CPU hardware for inference, making deployment via a Flask REST API viable
  for integration with a React Native mobile application. The system produces
  predictions in Sinhala Unicode text, rendered correctly using Pillow with
  the Nirmala UI font.

Contributions to Knowledge:

  This work makes contributions at multiple levels:

  At the systems level, the SSL Reader is the largest-vocabulary Sinhala Sign
  Language recognition system in the published literature (383 classes, compared
  to prior work at 10–50 classes), and the first SSL system to integrate
  facial expressions and body posture alongside hand gestures.

  At the architectural level, the Multi-Stream Fusion Model represents a novel
  combination of TCN and BiLSTM sub-networks with learned attention fusion
  for three-modality sign language recognition. The architecture is general and
  applicable to sign languages beyond SSL.

  At the methodological level, the systematic comparative evaluation of four
  architectures provides clear evidence that architectural separation of
  heterogeneous temporal features — not increased model capacity — is the
  dominant factor in multi-modal SLR under data-scarce conditions.

  At the practical level, the deployed system provides a working proof of
  concept for mobile-accessible SSL recognition, directly addressing a
  communication accessibility gap for the deaf community in Sri Lanka.

Future Directions:

The trajectory of improvements established by this work points clearly toward
the following future directions:

  (1) Dataset Expansion: Recording additional training videos (targeting 50+
      per class) with multiple signers is the highest-priority improvement. It
      would directly address the primary accuracy bottleneck and is a
      precondition for surpassing the 80%+ validation accuracy target.

  (2) Transfer Learning: Leveraging pre-trained representations from large-
      scale sign language datasets in related sign languages (American Sign
      Language, Australian Sign Language) could provide the model with richer
      initial feature representations that require less data to fine-tune for SSL.

  (3) Continuous Recognition: Extending from isolated sign recognition to
      continuous sign language recognition — where sign boundaries must be
      detected in continuous video — is the natural next step toward a fully
      practical translation system.

  (4) On-Device Deployment: Converting the PyTorch model to ONNX or
      PyTorch Mobile would allow fully on-device inference on smartphones,
      eliminating server dependency and reducing latency.

  (5) Community Integration: User studies with deaf SSL users in Sri Lanka are
      essential to evaluate real-world usability, identify misrecognised sign
      categories, and guide vocabulary expansion priorities.

The SSL Reader component, as part of the Intelligent Sinhala Sign Language
Communication Platform, represents a meaningful step toward reducing the
communication barriers faced by the deaf community in Sri Lanka. By combining
rigorous deep learning methodology with an application directly motivated by
real-world accessibility needs, this work demonstrates both the technical
feasibility and the social importance of computationally-assisted sign language
recognition in an under-resourced language context.

================================================================================
                               GLOSSARY
================================================================================

Attention Mechanism:
A neural network component that learns to assign different importance weights
to different elements of an input (e.g., different time steps in a sequence
or different feature streams). In the Multi-Stream Fusion Model, attention is
used to weight the contributions of the hand, face, and pose streams.

Batch Normalisation (BN):
A technique that normalises the activations within a mini-batch during training,
stabilising and accelerating the training of deep neural networks.

Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM):
An LSTM network that processes the input sequence in both forward (left-to-right)
and backward (right-to-left) directions, allowing each time step to be informed
by both past and future context.

Blendshape:
A weighted facial deformation parameter used in 3D face animation. MediaPipe
provides 52 blendshape scalar scores representing the activation level of
specific facial muscle groups (e.g., jaw_open, brow_inner_up). Used in this
work as expression intensity features.

Cosine Annealing with Warm Restarts:
A learning rate schedule where the learning rate follows a cosine curve from
its maximum to minimum value over a fixed period, then resets to the maximum.
The "warm restart" helps the optimiser escape local minima.

Dilation (in TCN):
A parameter of dilated convolution that controls the spacing between kernel
elements. Dilation = 2 means every other input element is skipped, effectively
doubling the receptive field without increasing the number of parameters.

Dropout:
A regularisation technique that randomly sets a fraction of activations to
zero during training, preventing the network from over-relying on specific
neurons and improving generalisation.

Feature Caching:
Storing extracted features to disk after first computation to avoid repeating
expensive extraction on subsequent training epochs.

Flask:
A lightweight Python web framework used to create the REST API backend for
the SSL Reader.

Label Smoothing:
A regularisation technique for classification tasks that replaces the
one-hot target distribution with a smoothed distribution, reducing the
model's overconfidence and improving generalisation.

Landmark:
A detected keypoint position on a body part (e.g., a fingertip, joint,
or facial feature) provided by pose estimation models such as MediaPipe.

MediaPipe:
Google's open-source framework for building real-time machine learning
perception pipelines. Used in this work for hand, face, and body pose
landmark extraction.

Multi-Stream Fusion Model:
The final model architecture proposed in this thesis, which routes each
modality (hands, face, pose) through a dedicated sub-network, then combines
the outputs using an attention mechanism.

React Native:
A cross-platform mobile application development framework used to build the
front-end mobile application that communicates with the SSL Reader API.

Root-Relative Normalisation:
A coordinate normalisation technique that expresses all landmarks relative
to a designated root landmark (e.g., the wrist for hands), removing
positional dependency on the signer's location in the camera frame.

Sinhala Sign Language (SSL):
The sign language used by the deaf community in Sri Lanka. SSL is a natural,
fully expressive language with its own grammar, distinct from spoken Sinhala.

Skeleton-Level Augmentation:
Data augmentation performed directly on landmark coordinate features rather
than pixel-level image data. Computationally efficient and preserves the
semantic validity of the landmark representation.

TCN (Temporal Convolutional Network):
A neural network architecture using causal dilated 1D convolutions for
sequence modelling. Supports fully parallelised training, unlike RNNs.

Transfer Learning:
Using a model pre-trained on a large dataset as a starting point for training
on a smaller, related dataset, leveraging learned representations to reduce
the amount of data required.

APPENDIX A — FULL FEATURE VECTOR SPECIFICATION
------------------------------------------------

The 457-dimensional feature vector per frame is composed as follows:

  Dimensions 0–62:    Left hand landmarks (21 × 3)
  Dimensions 63–125:  Right hand landmarks (21 × 3)
  Dimensions 126–305: Face key landmarks (60 × 3)
  Dimensions 306–357: Face blendshape scores (52 × 1)
  Dimensions 358–456: Pose landmarks (33 × 3)
  Total: 126 + 232 + 99 = 457 dimensions

Selected face landmark indices (within the full MediaPipe 468-landmark set):
  Eyes:      33, 133, 159, 145, 362, 263, 386, 374 (+ additional 4 points)
  Eyebrows:  70, 63, 105, 66, 107, 336, 296, 334, 293, 300
  Mouth:     61, 185, 40, 39, 37, 0, 267, 269, 270, 409, 291, 375, 321, 405
  Nose:      1, 2, 98, 327
  Face oval: 10, 338, 297, 332, 284, 251, 389, 356, 454, 323, 361, 288,
             397, 365, 379, 378, 400, 377, 152, 148


APPENDIX B — MODEL PARAMETER COUNTS
--------------------------------------

Multi-Stream Fusion Model parameter breakdown:

  Hand TCN Stream:
    Block 1: Conv1D(126→64)×2 + BN×2 + Residual(126→64) ≈ 88,832 params
    Block 2: Conv1D(64→128)×2 + BN×2 + Residual(64→128)  ≈ 164,352 params
    Block 3: Conv1D(128→128)×2 + BN×2                     ≈ 327,168 params
    Hand TCN subtotal                                      ≈ 580,352 params

  Face TCN Stream:
    Block 1: Conv1D(232→128)×2 + BN×2 + Residual          ≈ 327,680 params
    Block 2: Conv1D(128→256)×2 + BN×2 + Residual          ≈ 657,408 params
    Block 3: Conv1D(256→256)×2 + BN×2                     ≈ 656,384 params
    Face TCN subtotal                                      ≈ 1,641,472 params

  Pose BiLSTM Stream:
    BiLSTM(99, 32, bidirectional)                          ≈ 32,896 params
    Adaptive average pool (no params)

  Attention Fusion:
    Projection layers: Linear(128→512) + Linear(256→512) + Linear(64→512)
    Scoring network: Linear(512→256) + Linear(256→1)
    Fusion subtotal                                        ≈ 197,632 params

  Classifier Head:
    Linear(512→256) + ReLU + Linear(256→383)               ≈ 229,248 params

  Total approximate: ~2,520,000 parameters (~2.52M)

APPENDIX E — TRAINING COMMAND
-------------------------------

The model was trained using the following command:

  python train_mediapipe.py \
    --data_dir datasets/signVideo \
    --model_type multistream \
    --batch_size 16 \
    --max_epochs 120 \
    --patience 20 \
    --lr 0.001 \
    --weight_decay 1e-4 \
    --label_smoothing 0.1 \
    --dropout 0.3 \
    --output_dir components/ssl-reader/models

The training produces checkpoint_best.pth containing the model weights,
label_to_idx mapping, and model configuration, enabling inference to
reproduce the exact trained model configuration.
